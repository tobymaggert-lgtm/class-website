Explore

What Can a Graph Tell Us?

The following two data sets represent the number of hours students spent studying during the week.

Group A
2, 3, 3, 4, 4, 4, 5, 5, 5, 5, 6, 6, 6, 7, 7

Group B
1, 1, 2, 2, 3, 4, 5, 5, 6, 7, 8, 8, 9, 10, 12

  • Which group appears to have a more typical or consistent amount of studying?
  • Which group has more variability?
  • Where is the center of each distribution?
  • Are the distributions shaped similarly?
  • Does either distribution contain unusually high or low values?
  • Which group would be easier to predict a student's study time from?
  • What can you see in the graphs that would be difficult to notice from the lists alone?

Which group appears to have a more typical or consistent amount of studying? Group A appears more consistent because most values are concentrated between about 3 and 7 hours.

Which group has more variability? Group B has more variability because its values spread from 1 to 12 hours.

Where is the center of each distribution? Both have values near 5 hours, but Group A is much more concentrated around that center.

Are the distributions shaped similarly? No. Group A is fairly concentrated, while Group B is spread out with a longer right side.

Does either distribution contain unusually high or low values? The value of 12 in Group B may be considered unusually high.

Which group would be easier to predict a student's study time from? Group A, because its values are more concentrated.

What can you see in the graphs that would be difficult to notice from the lists? The graphs make center, spread, shape, clustering, and unusual values easier to see.

  • How spread out are the values?
  • Are there any gaps?
  • Are there any possible outliers?
  • What is the overall shape of the distribution?
  • What is easier to see in the dotplot than in the original list?

What value occurs most often? 7 hours occurs most often.

What value seems to represent the center? The distribution is centered around about 7 hours.

How spread out are the values? Values range from 4 to 10 hours, with most between about 5 and 9 hours.

Investigation 2: Histograms Group Data Into Intervals

Dotplots are useful for small data sets. When there are many observations, a histogram helps us see the overall shape.

Data representing test scores:

52, 58, 61, 63, 65, 66, 67, 68, 69, 70, 71, 72, 72, 73, 74, 75, 76, 77, 78, 79, 80, 81, 82, 83, 84, 85, 87, 88, 90, 94

  • Which interval contains the most scores?
  • Where is the center of the distribution?
  • How spread out are the scores?
  • Does the distribution appear symmetric or skewed?
  • Are there any gaps?
  • Are there any possible unusual values?
  • What information does the histogram make easier to see?

Which interval contains the most scores? The 70-79 interval contains the most scores.

Where is the center? Centered around the mid-to-upper 70s.

How spread out are the scores? Range from low 50s to mid 90s, with most concentrated between about 60 and 90.

Does it appear symmetric or skewed? Roughly mound-shaped, although the lower 52 and higher 94 create some spread on both sides.

Are there gaps? No major gaps in the middle.

Unusual values? Scores near 52 and 94 are farther from the main concentration and may deserve attention.

What does the histogram make easier to see? Where scores concentrate, the overall shape, and how scores spread across intervals.

Investigation 3: Change the Graph

Using the same test-score data, change the histogram interval width to see how it affects what we observe.

  • What changes when the intervals become wider?
  • What information becomes easier to see?
  • What information becomes harder to see?
  • Can two histograms of the same data look different?
  • Does one graph have to be "wrong"?
  • Why is choosing an appropriate graph part of statistical thinking?

What changes when intervals become wider? Wider intervals combine more observations into each bar, making the overall pattern simpler but hiding some detail.

What becomes easier to see? The broad overall shape of the distribution may become clearer.

What becomes harder to see? Smaller clusters, gaps, and differences between nearby values.

Can two histograms of the same data look different? Yes. Changing interval width or starting point changes the appearance.

Does one have to be "wrong"? No. Different reasonable choices can show the same data in different ways.

Why is choosing appropriate graphs part of statistical thinking? How data are displayed affects which patterns we notice.

Investigation 4: Compare Two Distributions

Team A Scores
72, 73, 74, 74, 75, 75, 75, 76, 76, 77, 78

Team B Scores
65, 68, 70, 72, 74, 75, 76, 78, 80, 82, 85

  • Which distribution has the greater center?
  • Which distribution has greater variability?
  • Which group is more consistent?
  • Which graph has values that are more clustered?
  • Are the distributions shaped similarly?
  • Are there possible outliers?
  • Which group would allow a more reliable prediction?
  • Why is comparing only the averages not enough?

Which has the greater center? Centers are fairly similar, both around the mid-70s.

Which has greater variability? Team B has much greater variability, ranging from 45 to 100.

Which is more consistent? Team A is more consistent, with values tightly clustered around the center.

More clustered? Team A has a clear cluster around the 70s.

Shaped similarly? No. Team A is tightly concentrated; Team B is spread out.

Possible outliers? Values near 45 and 100 in Team B may appear unusual.

More reliable prediction? Team A, because values are less variable.

Why not just compare averages? Groups can have similar centers but different spreads, shapes, and unusual values. The entire distribution gives more information.

Guiding Questions

  • Why is a graph often more useful than a list of quantitative data?

    A graph organizes the data visually so patterns, center, spread, clusters, gaps, and unusual observations are easier to see.

  • What does the shape of a distribution tell us?

    Shape describes the overall pattern, including whether it is symmetric, skewed, clustered, or contains gaps.

  • What does the center of a distribution represent?

    The center describes a typical or middle location of the data.

  • What does the spread of a distribution tell us?

    Spread describes how much observations vary from one another.

  • Why can two distributions have the same center but look very different?

    They may have different spreads, shapes, clusters, or unusual values.

  • What is a cluster?

    A cluster is a group of observations concentrated close together in one part of the distribution.

  • What is a gap?

    A gap is an interval or region where there are few or no observations.

  • What makes a value appear to be a possible outlier?

    A value may appear to be an outlier if it is much farther from the main group than most other values.

  • What does it mean for a distribution to be symmetric?

    A distribution is approximately symmetric when its left and right sides have roughly similar shapes around the center.

  • What does it mean for a distribution to be skewed?

    A distribution is skewed when one side extends farther than the other, creating a longer tail.

  • Why is a dotplot useful?

    A dotplot shows individual observations while also making the overall distribution easy to see.

  • Why is a histogram useful?

    A histogram groups quantitative values into intervals and helps reveal the overall shape of a larger distribution.

  • When might a histogram be more useful than a dotplot?

    A histogram is more useful when there are many observations or many possible numerical values.

  • Can changing the intervals of a histogram change its appearance?

    Yes. Different interval widths can reveal or hide details and make the same distribution appear somewhat different.

  • Why is it important to use the same scale when comparing two distributions?

    Using the same scale allows differences in center, spread, and shape to be compared fairly.

  • Why is looking only at the average sometimes misleading?

    An average does not describe the entire distribution. It may hide large differences in spread, shape, or unusual observations.

  • How can graphs help us understand variability?

    Graphs show how far observations spread from the center and whether values are tightly clustered or widely dispersed.

  • What should we look for when describing a quantitative distribution?

    Look for the shape, center, spread, clusters, gaps, and possible outliers.

The Big Idea

GraphsVisual representations of data that help reveal patterns and structure. reveal structureThe underlying organization and pattern of how data are arranged. and variabilityThe fact that data values differ from one another..

A list of numbers can contain a great deal of information, but the overall patterns are often difficult to see. A graph allows us to visualize the distributionThe overall pattern of how quantitative data are spread across possible values. of quantitative data.

When describing a distribution, we look for its shapeThe overall form or pattern of a distribution., centerA typical or middle location of the data., and spreadThe amount of variability in a distribution.. We also look for important features such as clustersGroups of observations that are concentrated close together., gapsRegions of a distribution where there are few or no observations., and possible outliersObservations that are unusually far from the rest of the data..

Two groups can have similar averages but very different distributions. Because of this, statistical thinking requires us to look beyond a single number and examine the overall pattern of the data.

Graphs help us ask:

Where are the data concentrated?

How much do the data vary?

Are there patterns or unusual observations?

Vocabulary:

SymmetricA distribution whose two sides are approximately similar around the center. and skewedA distribution with a longer tail extending farther in one direction. describe shape. A dotplotA graph that displays individual quantitative observations as dots along a number line. and histogramA graph that groups quantitative data into intervals and displays the frequency of observations in each interval. are useful visualization tools.

Numerical Summaries

Bellringer

Two Classes, Same Average

Class A: 72, 73, 74, 74, 75, 75, 75, 76, 76, 77, 78

Class B: 68, 70, 72, 74, 75, 75, 76, 78, 80, 82, 95

  • Which class appears to have the higher typical score?
  • Which class has more variability?
  • Could two groups have the same or similar mean but look very different?
  • Is one number enough to describe an entire distribution?

Objectives

  • I can interpret the meanThe arithmetic average of a set of numerical data. and medianThe middle value of a data set when the values are ordered from least to greatest. as measures of center.
  • I can explain when the mean and median may give different impressions of data.
  • I can describe standard deviationA measure of the typical distance of observations from the mean. as a measure of typical distance from the mean.
  • I can interpret the IQRThe interquartile range, or the difference between Q3 and Q1; it measures the spread of the middle 50% of the data. as a measure of spread for the middle 50% of the data.
  • I can interpret a five-number summaryThe minimum, Q1, median, Q3, and maximum values of a data set..
  • I can explain why numerical summaries do not describe everything about a distribution.
  • I can interpret a z-scoreA standardized value that tells how many standard deviations a data value is from the mean. conceptually.

Explore: Mean or Median?

Use a small data set with one unusually high value.

5, 6, 6, 7, 7, 8, 8, 9, 10, 30

  • What seems like a typical value?
  • Would the mean represent a typical observation?
  • Would the median represent a typical observation?
  • How does the value of 30 affect the mean?
  • Does it affect the median as much?

What seems like a typical value? Most values are between 5 and 10, so a typical value appears to be around 7 or 8.

Would the mean represent a typical observation? Not very well, because the unusually high value of 30 pulls the mean upward.

Would the median represent a typical observation? The median is much more representative because it is based on the middle of the ordered data.

How does the value of 30 affect the mean? It increases the mean substantially, making it less representative of the rest of the data.

Does it affect the median as much? No. The median stays near the center of the distribution and is much more resistant to the extreme value.

Investigation 1: Center

Compare two distributions that have similar centers but different shapes.

  • Which numerical summary better represents a typical value?
  • Does the graph change how useful the mean or median seems?
  • Why should we look at the distribution before relying on a summary number?

Which numerical summary better represents a typical value? The median may be more useful when the distribution is skewed or contains an extreme value. The mean is useful when the data are fairly balanced and not strongly affected by outliers.

Does the graph change how useful the mean or median seems? Yes. A graph shows whether the distribution is spread out, clustered, or skewed, which affects how much we trust a single center number.

Why should we look at the distribution before relying on a summary number? A single summary can hide important features such as skewness, gaps, clusters, and outliers.

Investigation 2: Measuring Variability

Standard deviationA measure of the typical distance of observations from the mean. describes the typical distance of observations from the mean.

It is a measure of spread. A small standard deviation means most data values are close to the mean. A large standard deviation means the data are more spread out.

Data Set A: 10, 11, 12, 12, 13, 13, 14, 14, 15, 15

Data Set B: 2, 6, 9, 11, 13, 14, 15, 16, 18, 21

  • Which distribution is more spread out?
  • Which has the larger standard deviation?
  • What does a small standard deviation suggest?
  • What does a large standard deviation suggest?
  • Can two distributions have the same mean but different standard deviations?

Which distribution is more spread out? Data Set B is more spread out because its values are much farther from the center.

Which has the larger standard deviation? Data Set B has the larger standard deviation.

What does a small standard deviation suggest? The data values are close to the mean.

What does a large standard deviation suggest? The data vary more and are more spread out from the center.

Can two distributions have the same mean but different standard deviations? Yes. Two groups can have the same average but different amounts of variability.

Investigation 3: Five-Number Summary and IQR

The five-number summaryThe minimum, Q1, median, Q3, and maximum values of a data set. includes:

  • Minimum
  • Q1
  • Median
  • Q3
  • Maximum

For example, a data set might have a five-number summary of:

Min = 12, Q1 = 18, Median = 24, Q3 = 30, Max = 38

The IQRThe interquartile range, or the distance between Q3 and Q1; it describes the spread of the middle 50% of the data. is calculated as:

IQR = Q3 − Q1

Here, IQR = 30 − 18 = 12.

  • What does the median tell us?
  • Where is the middle 50% of the data?
  • What does a larger IQR suggest?
  • Why might the IQR be useful when extreme values exist?

What does the median tell us? It tells us about the center of the data, with half the values below it and half above it.

Where is the middle 50% of the data? It lies between Q1 and Q3.

What does a larger IQR suggest? The middle 50% of the data are more spread out.

Why might the IQR be useful when extreme values exist? The IQR focuses on the middle of the distribution, so extreme values have less effect on it than they do on the range.

Guiding Questions

  • What is the difference between the mean and median?

    The mean is the arithmetic average, while the median is the middle value. The mean is more affected by extreme values, while the median is more resistant.

  • Why can an outlier affect the mean?

    An outlier adds a value that is far from the others, which can pull the average up or down.

  • Why is the median more resistant to extreme values?

    The median depends on the position of the middle value rather than the size of all values.

  • Can two distributions have the same mean but different variability?

    Yes. Two distributions can share the same center but differ in how spread out they are.

  • What does standard deviation describe?

    It describes the typical distance of values from the mean.

  • What does a small standard deviation suggest?

    Values tend to be close together and close to the mean.

  • What does a large standard deviation suggest?

    Values are more spread out and farther from the mean.

  • What information does the IQR describe?

    It describes the spread of the middle 50% of the data.

  • What are the five numbers in a five-number summary?

    Minimum, Q1, median, Q3, and maximum.

  • Why should we look at a graph as well as numerical summaries?

    Graphs reveal shape, clusters, gaps, and outliers that a single number can hide.

  • Can numerical summaries hide important features of a distribution?

    Yes. A summary can hide skewness, gaps, clusters, and unusual values.

The Big Idea

Numbers summarize distributions imperfectly.

Measures such as the meanThe arithmetic average of a set of data values., medianThe middle value of a data set when ordered from least to greatest., standard deviationA measure of the typical distance of observations from the mean., and IQRThe interquartile range, which measures the spread of the middle 50% of the data. help us describe important features of a distribution. The mean and median describe centerA typical or middle location of the data., while standard deviation and IQR help describe variabilityThe extent to which individual values differ from one another..

However, no single number tells the entire story. Two distributions can have the same center but very different spreads or shapes. Extreme values, clusters, gaps, and outliers may also be hidden by numerical summaries.

A z-scoreA standardized value that tells how many standard deviations a value is from the mean. allows us to describe a value relative to its distribution by considering both the mean and the standard deviation.

Statistical thinking requires us to use graphs and numerical summaries together.

Vocabulary:

meanThe arithmetic average of a data set., medianThe middle value in an ordered data set., standard deviationA measure of the typical distance of values from the mean., quartileA value that divides a data set into four equal parts., IQRThe spread of the middle 50% of the data, calculated as Q3 − Q1., five-number summaryThe minimum, Q1, median, Q3, and maximum values., .

Closure

Two students each score 85% on different tests.

Does that mean they performed equally well compared with their classmates? Explain why or why not.

Students should connect their answer to center, variability, and standardization.

One sentence to remember:

"A number can summarize a distribution, but it cannot replace understanding the distribution."
