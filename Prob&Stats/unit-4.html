Basic Probability Through Simulation

Bellringer

What is the chance of rolling a 6?

Predict the probability of rolling a 6 on a fair six-sided die. Explain how you could investigate your prediction by rolling a die repeatedly.

  • What is the theoretical probability of rolling a 6?
  • How could you investigate this probability experimentally?
  • Would 10 rolls always contain exactly the expected number of 6s?
  • What might happen if you used 100 or 1,000 rolls?

  • Roll a die many times, count the 6s, and divide the number of 6s by the total number of rolls.

  • With more rolls, the experimental probability will usually move closer to 1/6 and become more stable.

Objectives

  • Distinguish experimental probability from theoretical probability.
  • Calculate relative frequency.
  • Use simulation to investigate probability.
  • Explain the law of large numbers conceptually.
  • Recognize that experimental probabilities tend to stabilize toward theoretical probabilities as the number of trials increases.

Guiding Questions

  • How are experimental and theoretical probability different?
    • Theoretical probability comes from the structure of the chance process, while experimental probability comes from observed results.
  • Why can a small sample be far from the theoretical probability?
    • Random variation has a larger effect when there are only a few trials.
  • What tends to happen as the number of trials increases?
    • Experimental probabilities usually become less variable and tend to move closer to the theoretical probability.
  • Why does relative frequency tend to stabilize?
    • Each new trial has less influence on the overall proportion when the total number of trials is large.
  • What does the law of large numbers mean?
    • Over many repetitions, the relative frequency of an event tends to get closer to its theoretical probability.
  • Does the law of large numbers guarantee an exact probability after a certain number of trials?
    • No. It describes a long-run tendency, not a guarantee that one simulation will equal the theoretical probability.

The Big Idea

Probability describes long-run behavior.

Experimental probability can vary substantially in a small number of trials. As the number of trials becomes large, relative frequencies tend to stabilize near the theoretical probability.

The law of large numbers describes this long-run pattern. It does not promise that every short experiment, or even one large experiment, will produce the exact theoretical probability.

Apply

  1. A bag has 3 red marbles and 2 blue marbles. Find the theoretical probability of drawing a red marble.
  2. A coin is flipped 80 times and lands heads 46 times. Calculate the experimental probability of heads and compare it with the theoretical probability.
  3. A spinner has 4 equal sections. In 20 spins, the blue section appears 9 times. Explain one reason the experimental probability differs from the theoretical probability.
  4. A die is rolled 1,000 times and a 6 appears 171 times. Describe how the law of large numbers applies.
  • The theoretical probability of red is 3/5, or 0.6.
  • The experimental probability is 46/80 = 0.575, which is close to the theoretical probability of 0.5 but not exactly equal.
  • The difference can be caused by random variation in the small number of spins.
  • The experimental probability is 171/1,000 = 0.171, which is close to 1/6, or about 0.167; this illustrates long-run stabilization without guaranteeing an exact match.

Closure

Explain, in your own words, why probability is better understood as long-run behavior rather than a guarantee about what happens in a small number of trials.

Complete: “Probability is the study of __________ over many repetitions.”

  • Probability describes the patterns and relative frequencies that tend to appear over many repetitions, but random variation means it cannot guarantee the result of a small experiment.

Conditional Probability and Association

Bellringer

Does participation relate to meeting a goal?

  • What are the row and column totals?
  • What is the joint probability that a student participated and met the goal?
  • What changes when you restrict attention to students who participated?
  • The participated and goal-met cell contains 29 students.

Objectives

  • Read and interpret two-way tables.
  • Calculate conditional probabilities.
  • Explain conditional probability as a restricted sample space.
  • Use visual representations to compare conditional probabilities.
  • Determine conceptually whether two events appear independent.

Explore

Let A mean “the student met the goal” and B mean “the student participated.”

Find the total number of observations. Find the probability of A. Find the probability of B. Find the probability of A and B.

Restrict your attention to the cases where B occurs. Calculate the proportion of those cases where A also occurs.

Compare this conditional probability with the overall probability of A. Explain what changed when you conditioned on B.

P(A|B) means the probability of A among the cases where B has occurred.

P(B) = 0.59, P(A and B) = 0.29, and P(A|B) = P(A and B) P(B) = 0.29/0.59 ≈ 0.49

The highlighted portion of the visualization is A and B: the students who participated and met the goal. It is part of the group where B occurred.

  • What is the overall probability of meeting the goal?
  • What is the probability of meeting the goal among students who participated?
  • Why is 59 the denominator for the conditional probability?
  • How does the restricted probability compare with the overall probability?
  • The overall probability of meeting the goal is 50/100 = 0.50.
  • The probability of meeting the goal among students who participated is 29/59 ≈ 0.49.
  • The denominator is 59 because there are 59 students in the group who participated.
  • The restricted probability is close to the overall probability, so conditioning on participation changes the proportion only slightly in this example.

Guiding Questions

  • What does “given that B occurred” mean?
    • It means we focus only on the observations where B occurred.
  • Why does the denominator change when calculating P(A|B)?
    • The denominator changes because the sample space is restricted to the cases where B occurred.
  • How is conditional probability different from P(A)?
    • P(A) uses all observations, while P(A|B) uses only observations where B occurred.
  • When does knowing B change the probability of A?
    • Knowing B changes the probability of A when the proportion of A among B cases differs from the overall proportion of A.
  • What would it look like if A and B were independent?
    • If A and B were independent, the probability of A would stay about the same whether or not we knew B.
  • How can a two-way table help reveal association?
    • A two-way table lets us compare proportions within groups and see whether the variables appear related.

The Big Idea

Probabilities can depend on conditions.

Conditional probability changes the sample space. Instead of considering all observations, focus only on those satisfying the condition.

If the conditional probability of A changes depending on whether B occurred, the variables are associated. If the relevant probabilities remain about the same, the events appear independent.

Apply

Use each two-way table. Find the joint probability. Calculate the conditional probability. Compare P(A) and P(A|B).

  1. A table has 18 students in both the music and passed cells out of 60 students. What is the joint probability of music and passed?
  2. Of 40 students who exercised, 28 slept at least 8 hours. What is the conditional probability of sleeping at least 8 hours given exercise?
  3. In a table, P(A) = 0.40 and P(A|B) = 0.65. Is there evidence that A and B are associated?
  4. In a table, P(A) = 0.50 and P(A|B) = 0.50. What does this suggest?
  • The joint probability is 18/60 = 0.30.
  • The conditional probability is 28/40 = 0.70.
  • Yes. The difference between 0.40 and 0.65 suggests that knowing B changes the probability of A, which is evidence of association.
  • This is consistent with independence because the conditional probability is the same as the overall probability, although a full conclusion depends on the data and context.

Closure

Explain: “What does P(A|B) mean, and why does the condition B change the group we are considering?”

  • P(A|B) means the probability of A among the cases where B occurred; condition B changes the group because we ignore cases where B did not occur.

Lesson 17

Normal Distributions and Standardization

Bellringer

How unusual is a test score?

A test has a mean score of 75 and a standard deviation of 10.

  • What does it mean for a score to be above or below the mean?
  • Would a score 2 points above the mean or 20 points above the mean be more unusual?
  • How could you compare scores from different tests or classes?
  • A score above the mean is higher than typical, while a score below the mean is lower than typical.
  • A score 20 points above the mean is more unusual because it is farther from the mean in standard-deviation units.
  • Use z-scores to describe each score's location relative to its own mean and standard deviation.

Objectives

  • Recognize the general shape and features of a normal distribution.
  • Interpret a z-score as the number of standard deviations a value is from the mean.
  • Calculate and interpret z-scores.
  • Connect z-scores to percentiles.
  • Use a normal model to estimate proportions conceptually.

Explore

A normal distribution is approximately symmetric and mound-shaped. Values near the mean are common; values far from the mean are less common.

Identify the mean and standard deviation. Locate the given value on the distribution. Determine whether the value is above or below the mean.

Use this example: mean = 75, standard deviation = 10, and score = 87.

Calculate the z-score: z = x - μσ = 87 - 7510 = 1.2

Explain what the z-score means in context. A score of 87 is approximately 1.2 standard deviations above the mean, which is around the 88.5th percentile in a normal model.

Compare two z-scores. Determine which value is more unusual.

  • What does a z-score of 1.2 tell us about the score of 87?
  • Is a score with z = 1.2 above or below the mean?
  • Which is farther from its mean: z = 0.5 or z = 2.0?
  • What does the percentile tell us?
  • The score of 87 is 1.2 standard deviations above its distribution's mean.
  • A positive z-score means the value is above the mean.
  • z = 2.0 is farther from the mean and is more unusual.
  • The percentile estimates the percentage of values at or below the given value.

Guiding Questions

  • What does a z-score of 0 mean?
    • The value is exactly at the mean.
  • What does a positive z-score mean?
    • The value is above the mean.
  • What does a negative z-score mean?
    • The value is below the mean.
  • What does a z-score of 1.2 mean in context?
    • The value is 1.2 standard deviations above its distribution's mean.
  • Which is more unusual: z = 0.5 or z = 2.0?
    • z = 2.0 is more unusual because it is farther from the mean.
  • What does the percentile tell us?
    • It estimates the percentage of values at or below a given value.
  • Can two different raw scores have the same z-score?
    • Yes, if they are the same number of standard deviations from their respective means.
  • Why are z-scores useful for comparing values from different distributions?
    • They put values from different scales into the same standard-deviation units.

The Big Idea

Some processes follow predictable patterns.

A z-score standardizes a value by describing its location relative to the mean in standard-deviation units.

A z-score tells how far a value is from the mean, uses standard deviations as the unit, is positive above the mean, negative below the mean, and allows values from different distributions to be compared.

A normal model helps us estimate proportions conceptually: values near the center are common, while values far from the mean are less common.

Apply

Calculate the z-score. Interpret the result. Compare the values relative to their own distributions.

  1. A score of 70 comes from a distribution with mean 65 and standard deviation 5. Find and interpret its z-score.
  2. A score of 42 comes from a distribution with mean 50 and standard deviation 4. Is its z-score positive, negative, or zero?
  3. Alex scores 84 on a test with mean 75 and standard deviation 6. Jordan scores 91 on a test with mean 80 and standard deviation 10. Who performed better relative to their own distribution?
  4. About what percentile corresponds to z = 0?
  5. Why can a normal model estimate proportions without guaranteeing the result for every individual?
  • The z-score is (70 - 65)/5 = 1, so the score is 1 standard deviation above its mean.
  • The z-score is (42 - 50)/4 = -2, so it is negative and the score is 2 standard deviations below the mean.
  • Alex performed better relative to the distribution because Alex's z-score is 1.5, while Jordan's is 1.1.
  • z = 0 corresponds to approximately the 50th percentile because it is at the mean.
  • A normal model describes patterns and proportions across many observations, not a guarantee about one individual outcome.

Closure

What does a z-score tell us that the original score does not?

  • A z-score tells us how far the value is from its mean in standard-deviation units, making its relative position easier to interpret and compare.
