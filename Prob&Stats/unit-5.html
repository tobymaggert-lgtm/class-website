Lesson 19

Combinations, Permutations, and Standard Deviation

Big Idea

Some statistical calculations depend on counting possible arrangements, while standard deviation measures how much data vary from the mean.

First we will count selections and arrangements. Then we will organize data in a table to measure its spread.

Learning Goals

  • Decide whether order matters and distinguish combinations from permutations.
  • Use factorial notation and calculate simple combinations and permutations by hand.
  • Organize data in a table to calculate and interpret sample standard deviation.
  • Connect combinations to the nCr notation used in the next lesson.

Part 1: Combinations and Permutations

Permutation: order matters. Imagine choosing 3 students for 1st, 2nd, and 3rd place. Alex, Bri, Cam is a different result from Bri, Alex, Cam because the places changed.

Combination: order does not matter. If those same students form a committee, Alex, Bri, Cam is the same committee as Bri, Alex, Cam.

Podium: 1st Alex, 2nd Bri, 3rd Cam is a different arrangement from 1st Bri, 2nd Alex, 3rd Cam.

Committee: Alex + Bri + Cam is the same group no matter who is named first.

  • Three students are selected to receive gold, silver, and bronze. Does order matter?
    • Yes. The medals are different places, so changing the order changes the result. This is a permutation.
  • Three students are selected for a committee. Does order matter?
    • No. The same three people make the same committee in any order. This is a combination.
Factorials and formulas

nPr = n! / (n − r)! Order matters.

nCr = n! / [r!(n − r)!] Order does not matter.

  • n = total number of objects
  • r = number selected
  • ! means factorial: multiply that whole number by every positive integer below it. For example, 4! = 4 · 3 · 2 · 1 = 24.

For a quick calculation, 5P2 = 5 · 4 = 20. There are 5 choices for the first position and 4 remaining choices for the second.

  • Calculate 5P2.
    • 5P2 = 5 · 4 = 20.
  • Calculate 6P3.
    • 6P3 = 6 · 5 · 4 = 120.
  • Calculate 5C2.
    • 5C2 = (5 · 4) / (2 · 1) = 10.
  • Calculate 6C3.
    • 6C3 = (6 · 5 · 4) / (3 · 2 · 1) = 20.

Preview for Lesson 20: You will use combinations again in the next lesson when calculating binomial probabilities. The nCr part of the binomial formula counts how many different ways the successes can be arranged.

Guiding Questions

  1. How do you know whether to use a combination or a permutation?
    • Ask whether changing the order creates a different result. If it does, use a permutation; if not, use a combination.
  2. Why does order matter for a permutation?
    • Each position or role is different, so the same objects in a different order count as a different arrangement.
  3. Why does order not matter for a combination?
    • A combination counts only which objects were selected. Reordering the same group does not create a new group.
  4. What does nCr represent?
    • It counts the number of ways to select r objects from n when order does not matter.
  5. What does standard deviation measure?
    • It describes the typical distance of data values from the mean and therefore how spread out the data are.
  6. Why do we subtract the mean from each data value?
    • The difference tells how far and in which direction each value is from the center of the data.
  7. Why do we square the deviations?
    • Squaring makes negative and positive differences contribute positively and gives larger differences more weight.
  8. How does the standard-deviation table organize the calculation?
    • It lists each value, its difference from the mean, and its squared difference so we can add the squares, divide by n − 1, and take the square root.

Apply

  1. A club chooses 2 of 5 students for a committee. Decide whether this is a combination or permutation, then calculate the number of groups.
    • Order does not matter, so use a combination: 5C2 = 10 committees.
  2. Four runners finish in first through fourth place from a group of 6. Decide whether this is a combination or permutation, then calculate the number of outcomes.
    • Order matters because places differ, so use a permutation: 6P4 = 6 · 5 · 4 · 3 = 360 outcomes.

The Big Idea

Counting methods tell us how many arrangements or selections are possible. Standard deviation describes how far data values typically spread from their mean.

Permutations count different orders; combinations count groups without regard to order. For data, a standard-deviation table turns each distance from the mean into a squared contribution to the overall spread.

Lesson 20

Binomial Distribution

Big Idea

A binomial distribution describes the number of successes we would expect when an experiment has a fixed number of trials and a constant probability of success.

We will calculate what chance alone can produce when the same two-outcome process is repeated.

Learning Goals

  • Identify whether a situation is binomial using all four conditions.
  • Calculate the probability of exactly r successes and of a small range of successes.
  • Find and interpret expected successes using np.
  • Connect a binomial model to a null hypothesis and what chance alone would produce.

Explore: What Could Chance Produce?

Assume a basketball player has a 50% chance of making each free throw. Imagine 10 free throws.

  • What numbers of made shots are possible?
    • Any whole number from 0 through 10 makes is possible.
  • Which numbers seem most likely? Which numbers seem unusual?
    • Results near 5 makes seem most likely; very low or high totals such as 0 or 10 seem unusual, but remain possible.
  • How could simulation help investigate what chance would produce?
    • Simulate 10 independent attempts with a 0.50 chance of a make, repeat the group many times, and record the number of makes each time.

From simulation to calculation: Instead of simulating thousands of trials to see what chance would produce, we can sometimes calculate those probabilities directly.

Hand calculation: exactly 3 successes in 5 trials

Let success mean making a free throw. Here, n = 5 trials, r = 3 successes, and p = 0.5.

P(3) = 5C3 · (0.5)3 · (1 − 0.5)5−3

= 10 · 0.125 · 0.25 = 0.3125

There is a 31.25% chance of exactly 3 makes in 5 attempts under these assumptions.

Binomial Model and Formula

P(r) = nCr · pr · qn−r

Expected number of successes = np

  • n = number of trials
  • r = number of successes whose probability we want
  • p = probability of success on each trial
  • q = probability of failure on each trial
  • nCr = number of ways to choose r successes from n trials

The four conditions are a fixed number of trials, two outcomes per trial, independent trials, and constant probability of success.

Independent trialsThe outcome of one trial does not change the probabilities for another trial. keep the probability at p each time. The nCr counts how many different trial-orders can produce exactly r successes.

    Hand Calculation Practice

    Identify n, x, and p before substituting.

    1. Four independent trials have success probability 0.5. Find the probability of exactly 1 success.
      • n = 4, x = 1, p = 0.5. P(1) = 4C1(0.5)1(0.5)3 = 4(0.0625) = 0.25.
    2. In 4 trials with p = 0.5, find the probability of 2 or 3 successes by adding individual probabilities.
      • P(2) + P(3) = 4C2(0.5)4 + 4C3(0.5)4 = 6/16 + 4/16 = 10/16 = 0.625.
    3. A player makes 25% of shots. In 8 independent attempts, how many successes should we expect?
      • n = 8 and p = 0.25, so np = 8(0.25) = 2 successes.

    Guiding Questions

    1. What four conditions must be true for a setting to be binomial?
      • A fixed number of trials, two outcomes per trial, independent trials, and a constant probability of success.
    2. A bag has 4 red and 6 blue tiles. You draw 3 without replacement and count red tiles. Is this binomial? Which condition fails?
      • No. Draws are dependent and the chance of red changes after each draw, so independence and constant probability fail.
    3. In the formula, what do n, x, and p represent?
      • n is the fixed number of trials, x is the number of successes of interest, and p is the success probability on each trial.
    4. Why does the formula include nCr?
      • It counts the different orders in which exactly x successes can occur among n trials.
    5. For 5 trials with p = 0.5, what is the probability of exactly 3 successes?
      • 5C3(0.5)3(0.5)2 = 10/32 = 0.3125, or 31.25%.
    6. For that same model, how would you find the probability of 2 or 3 successes?
      • Find P(2) and P(3), then add: 10/32 + 10/32 = 0.625.
    7. What does np tell us? Interpret it for 10 trials with p = 0.5.
      • np is the expected number of successes. Here it is 10(0.5) = 5 successes on average over many groups, not necessarily exactly 5 each time.
    8. How does a binomial distribution connect a null hypothesis to simulations from Lessons 9–14?
      • If the null hypothesis specifies a success rate, the binomial model calculates how likely different counts are just from chance, like the chance model explored through repeated simulations.

    The Big Idea

    A binomial distribution tells us what numbers of successes are reasonably expected when the same two-outcome process is repeated under consistent conditions.

    In Lessons 9–14, simulations showed what results were common or unusual if a null hypothesis were true. If the null hypothesis says a certain success rate is true, a binomial distribution tells us how likely different success counts would be just from chance. Calculation and simulation both ask what results a chance model can produce.

    Apply

    For each situation, decide whether it is binomial. When it is, identify n, p, and the requested x, then calculate and interpret.

    1. A player makes each free throw with probability 0.7. For 6 independent free throws, find the probability of exactly 4 makes and interpret it.
      • This is binomial: fixed n = 6, two outcomes, independent attempts, constant p = 0.7. For x = 4, P(4) = 6C4(0.7)4(0.3)2 = 0.3241. Under this model, there is about a 32.4% chance the player makes exactly 4 of these 6 free throws.
    2. A seed has a 0.2 chance of sprouting. If 5 seeds sprout independently, find the probability exactly 2 sprout.
      • This is binomial with n = 5, x = 2, and p = 0.2. P(2) = 5C2(0.2)2(0.8)3 = 10(0.04)(0.512) = 0.2048. About 20.48% of groups of 5 would have exactly 2 sprouts.
    3. A player makes 50% of free throws. Over 10 independent attempts, what is the expected number of makes? Interpret it.
      • n = 10 and p = 0.5, so np = 10(0.5) = 5 makes on average over many sets of 10 attempts. One set need not have exactly 5.
    4. You draw 5 cards without replacement from a standard deck and count the hearts. Is this binomial? Explain which condition fails.
      • This is not binomial. Draws without replacement are dependent and the probability of a heart changes each time.

    Closure

    How is a binomial calculation similar to the simulations we used earlier to understand statistical significance?

    Explain in your own words what is held constant, what outcomes are counted, and what an unusual result suggests.

    • Both start with a chance model, such as a null-hypothesis success rate, and ask what results could happen if it were true. Simulation estimates how often results occur by repeating the process; a binomial calculation finds those probabilities directly. A very small probability marks a result as unusual under the model, not impossible.

    Lesson 21

    Introduction to the Normal Distribution & Empirical Rule

    Big Idea

    Many quantitative measurements have a roughly symmetric, bell-shaped distribution, and the normal model gives us a predictable way to describe how values are distributed.

    Today we will describe a distribution using its center and spread, then estimate how much data lie near the mean.

    Learning Goals

    • Recognize a distribution that is approximately bell-shaped and symmetric.
    • Explain how the mean describes center and standard deviation describes spread.
    • Use the Empirical Rule (68–95–99.7 rule) to estimate percentages and values.
    • Recognize that the rule is an approximation for distributions that are reasonably normal.

    Explore: What Does a Normal Distribution Look Like?

    Compare these simple distribution sketches. Which one looks approximately normal?

    1. Which sketch is most symmetric and bell-shaped?
      • Distribution A is approximately symmetric with one central peak, so it is the best match for a normal shape.
    2. Where is the center of Distribution A, and how could you describe its spread?
      • The center is near the middle of the horizontal axis, where the peak is. Spread describes how broadly values fan out around that center.
    3. Which values might appear unusual in an approximately normal distribution?
      • Values far out in either tail, several standard deviations from the mean, are less common and may appear unusual.

    For a normal model, the mean marks the center. The standard deviation describes the spread: a larger standard deviation makes the curve wider, while a smaller one makes it narrower.

    The Empirical Rule

    For a distribution that is reasonably normal, these approximate percentages fall within each distance from the mean:

    68%
    within 1 standard deviation

    95%
    within 2 standard deviations

    99.7%
    within 3 standard deviations

    Outside those intervals: about 32% outside ±1 SD, 5% outside ±2 SD, and 0.3% outside ±3 SD.

    Outside percentages combine both tails. If the distribution is symmetric, about half of each outside percentage lies in either tail.

    Approximate does not mean exact. Use the Empirical Rule only when the distribution is reasonably bell-shaped and symmetric. It is not a rule for every data set.

    Guiding Questions

    1. What shape and symmetry describe a distribution that is approximately normal?
      • It is roughly bell-shaped, with one central peak and sides that are approximately mirror images.
    2. Where is the center of a normal distribution?
      • The mean is at the center of the normal distribution.
    3. What does the standard deviation tell us about the distribution?
      • It describes the spread of values around the mean; a larger standard deviation means a wider distribution.
    4. What does the 68% in the Empirical Rule mean?
      • About 68% of values in a reasonably normal distribution fall between one standard deviation below and one above the mean.
    5. What does the 95% mean?
      • About 95% of values fall within two standard deviations of the mean.
    6. What does the 99.7% mean?
      • About 99.7% of values fall within three standard deviations of the mean.
    7. How do you find values one, two, or three standard deviations from the mean?
      • Multiply the standard deviation by 1, 2, or 3, then add that amount to the mean for values above it or subtract it for values below it.
    8. Why is the Empirical Rule only an approximation?
      • Real distributions are not perfectly normal, and the rule describes approximate percentages only for distributions that are reasonably bell-shaped and symmetric.

    The Big Idea

    A normal distribution is approximately symmetric and bell-shaped. The mean tells us where the distribution is centered, while the standard deviation tells us how spread out it is. The Empirical Rule lets us estimate how much data fall within 1, 2, and 3 standard deviations of the mean.

    The rule is useful when the distribution is reasonably normal, but it is not exact and should not be applied to strongly skewed or irregular data.

    Apply

    Use the Empirical Rule only for the contexts described as reasonably normal. A value more than 2 standard deviations from the mean is relatively unusual; beyond 3 standard deviations is very unusual.

    1. Adult heights in a group are reasonably normal with mean 170 cm and standard deviation 7 cm. Find the values 1, 2, and 3 standard deviations from the mean, then estimate the percentage between 156 cm and 184 cm.
      • The 1-SD values are 163 and 177 cm; 2-SD values are 156 and 184 cm; 3-SD values are 149 and 191 cm. The interval 156–184 cm is within 2 SD, so it contains about 95% of the heights.
    2. Test scores are reasonably normal with mean 75 points and standard deviation 8 points. Estimate the percentage between 67 and 83, and decide whether a score of 99 is unusual.
      • 67 and 83 are 1 SD from the mean, so about 68% of scores fall in that interval. A score of 99 is 3 SD above the mean (75 + 3 × 8), so it is very unusual under this model.
    3. Measurement errors are reasonably normal with mean 0 mm and standard deviation 2 mm. Estimate the percentage of errors between −4 mm and 4 mm, and the percentage greater than 4 mm.
      • −4 and 4 are 2 SD from the mean, so about 95% of errors are in that interval. About 5% is outside both ends, so approximately 2.5% is greater than 4 mm.
    4. A machine's fill amounts are reasonably normal with mean 500 mL and standard deviation 10 mL. Find the 3-SD interval and identify whether 525 mL is unusual.
      • The 3-SD interval is 500 ± 30, or 470 to 530 mL. A fill of 525 mL is 2.5 SD above the mean, so it is uncommon and lies beyond the 2-SD interval, though still within 3 SD.
    5. Household incomes in a region have many values clustered at the low end and a long tail toward very high incomes. Should the Empirical Rule be used? Explain.
      • No. This distribution is strongly right-skewed rather than approximately symmetric and bell-shaped, so the Empirical Rule percentages are not appropriate.

    Closure

    If you know the mean and standard deviation of a reasonably normal distribution, what can you predict about where most of the data will fall?

    • You can estimate that about 68% of values fall within 1 standard deviation, 95% within 2, and 99.7% within 3 standard deviations of the mean.
