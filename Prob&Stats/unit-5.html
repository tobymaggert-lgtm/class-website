Lesson 19

Combinations, Permutations, and Standard Deviation

Big Idea

Some statistical calculations depend on counting possible arrangements, while standard deviation measures how much data vary from the mean.

First we will count selections and arrangements. Then we will organize data in a table to measure its spread.

Learning Goals

  • Decide whether order matters and distinguish combinations from permutations.
  • Use factorial notation and calculate simple combinations and permutations by hand.
  • Organize data in a table to calculate and interpret sample standard deviation.
  • Connect combinations to the nCr notation used in the next lesson.

Part 1: Combinations and Permutations

Permutation: order matters. Imagine choosing 3 students for 1st, 2nd, and 3rd place. Alex, Bri, Cam is a different result from Bri, Alex, Cam because the places changed.

Combination: order does not matter. If those same students form a committee, Alex, Bri, Cam is the same committee as Bri, Alex, Cam.

Podium: 1st Alex, 2nd Bri, 3rd Cam is a different arrangement from 1st Bri, 2nd Alex, 3rd Cam.

Committee: Alex + Bri + Cam is the same group no matter who is named first.

  • Three students are selected to receive gold, silver, and bronze. Does order matter?
    • Yes. The medals are different places, so changing the order changes the result. This is a permutation.
  • Three students are selected for a committee. Does order matter?
    • No. The same three people make the same committee in any order. This is a combination.
Factorials and formulas

nPr = n! / (n − r)! Order matters.

nCr = n! / [r!(n − r)!] Order does not matter.

  • n = total number of objects
  • r = number selected
  • ! means factorial: multiply that whole number by every positive integer below it. For example, 4! = 4 · 3 · 2 · 1 = 24.

For a quick calculation, 5P2 = 5 · 4 = 20. There are 5 choices for the first position and 4 remaining choices for the second.

  • Calculate 5P2.
    • 5P2 = 5 · 4 = 20.
  • Calculate 6P3.
    • 6P3 = 6 · 5 · 4 = 120.
  • Calculate 5C2.
    • 5C2 = (5 · 4) / (2 · 1) = 10.
  • Calculate 6C3.
    • 6C3 = (6 · 5 · 4) / (3 · 2 · 1) = 20.

Preview for Lesson 20: You will use combinations again in the next lesson when calculating binomial probabilities. The nCr part of the binomial formula counts how many different ways the successes can be arranged.

Guiding Questions

  1. How do you know whether to use a combination or a permutation?
    • Ask whether changing the order creates a different result. If it does, use a permutation; if not, use a combination.
  2. Why does order matter for a permutation?
    • Each position or role is different, so the same objects in a different order count as a different arrangement.
  3. Why does order not matter for a combination?
    • A combination counts only which objects were selected. Reordering the same group does not create a new group.
  4. What does nCr represent?
    • It counts the number of ways to select r objects from n when order does not matter.
  5. What does standard deviation measure?
    • It describes the typical distance of data values from the mean and therefore how spread out the data are.
  6. Why do we subtract the mean from each data value?
    • The difference tells how far and in which direction each value is from the center of the data.
  7. Why do we square the deviations?
    • Squaring makes negative and positive differences contribute positively and gives larger differences more weight.
  8. How does the standard-deviation table organize the calculation?
    • It lists each value, its difference from the mean, and its squared difference so we can add the squares, divide by n − 1, and take the square root.

Apply

  1. A club chooses 2 of 5 students for a committee. Decide whether this is a combination or permutation, then calculate the number of groups.
    • Order does not matter, so use a combination: 5C2 = 10 committees.
  2. Four runners finish in first through fourth place from a group of 6. Decide whether this is a combination or permutation, then calculate the number of outcomes.
    • Order matters because places differ, so use a permutation: 6P4 = 6 · 5 · 4 · 3 = 360 outcomes.

The Big Idea

Counting methods tell us how many arrangements or selections are possible. Standard deviation describes how far data values typically spread from their mean.

Permutations count different orders; combinations count groups without regard to order. For data, a standard-deviation table turns each distance from the mean into a squared contribution to the overall spread.

Lesson 20

Binomial Distribution

Big Idea

A binomial distribution describes the number of successes we would expect when an experiment has a fixed number of trials and a constant probability of success.

We will calculate what chance alone can produce when the same two-outcome process is repeated.

Learning Goals

  • Identify whether a situation is binomial using all four conditions.
  • Calculate the probability of exactly r successes and of a small range of successes.
  • Find and interpret expected successes using np.
  • Connect a binomial model to a null hypothesis and what chance alone would produce.

Explore: What Could Chance Produce?

Assume a basketball player has a 50% chance of making each free throw. Imagine 10 free throws.

  • What numbers of made shots are possible?
    • Any whole number from 0 through 10 makes is possible.
  • Which numbers seem most likely? Which numbers seem unusual?
    • Results near 5 makes seem most likely; very low or high totals such as 0 or 10 seem unusual, but remain possible.
  • How could simulation help investigate what chance would produce?
    • Simulate 10 independent attempts with a 0.50 chance of a make, repeat the group many times, and record the number of makes each time.

From simulation to calculation: Instead of simulating thousands of trials to see what chance would produce, we can sometimes calculate those probabilities directly.

Hand calculation: exactly 3 successes in 5 trials

Let success mean making a free throw. Here, n = 5 trials, r = 3 successes, and p = 0.5.

P(3) = 5C3 · (0.5)3 · (1 − 0.5)5−3

= 10 · 0.125 · 0.25 = 0.3125

There is a 31.25% chance of exactly 3 makes in 5 attempts under these assumptions.

Binomial Model and Formula

P(r) = nCr · pr · qn−r

Expected number of successes = np

  • n = number of trials
  • r = number of successes whose probability we want
  • p = probability of success on each trial
  • q = probability of failure on each trial
  • nCr = number of ways to choose r successes from n trials

The four conditions are a fixed number of trials, two outcomes per trial, independent trials, and constant probability of success.

Independent trialsThe outcome of one trial does not change the probabilities for another trial. keep the probability at p each time. The nCr counts how many different trial-orders can produce exactly r successes.

    Hand Calculation Practice

    Identify n, x, and p before substituting.

    1. Four independent trials have success probability 0.5. Find the probability of exactly 1 success.
      • n = 4, x = 1, p = 0.5. P(1) = 4C1(0.5)1(0.5)3 = 4(0.0625) = 0.25.
    2. In 4 trials with p = 0.5, find the probability of 2 or 3 successes by adding individual probabilities.
      • P(2) + P(3) = 4C2(0.5)4 + 4C3(0.5)4 = 6/16 + 4/16 = 10/16 = 0.625.
    3. A player makes 25% of shots. In 8 independent attempts, how many successes should we expect?
      • n = 8 and p = 0.25, so np = 8(0.25) = 2 successes.

    Guiding Questions

    1. What four conditions must be true for a setting to be binomial?
      • A fixed number of trials, two outcomes per trial, independent trials, and a constant probability of success.
    2. A bag has 4 red and 6 blue tiles. You draw 3 without replacement and count red tiles. Is this binomial? Which condition fails?
      • No. Draws are dependent and the chance of red changes after each draw, so independence and constant probability fail.
    3. In the formula, what do n, x, and p represent?
      • n is the fixed number of trials, x is the number of successes of interest, and p is the success probability on each trial.
    4. Why does the formula include nCr?
      • It counts the different orders in which exactly x successes can occur among n trials.
    5. For 5 trials with p = 0.5, what is the probability of exactly 3 successes?
      • 5C3(0.5)3(0.5)2 = 10/32 = 0.3125, or 31.25%.
    6. For that same model, how would you find the probability of 2 or 3 successes?
      • Find P(2) and P(3), then add: 10/32 + 10/32 = 0.625.
    7. What does np tell us? Interpret it for 10 trials with p = 0.5.
      • np is the expected number of successes. Here it is 10(0.5) = 5 successes on average over many groups, not necessarily exactly 5 each time.
    8. How does a binomial distribution connect a null hypothesis to simulations from Lessons 9–14?
      • If the null hypothesis specifies a success rate, the binomial model calculates how likely different counts are just from chance, like the chance model explored through repeated simulations.

    The Big Idea

    A binomial distribution tells us what numbers of successes are reasonably expected when the same two-outcome process is repeated under consistent conditions.

    In Lessons 9–14, simulations showed what results were common or unusual if a null hypothesis were true. If the null hypothesis says a certain success rate is true, a binomial distribution tells us how likely different success counts would be just from chance. Calculation and simulation both ask what results a chance model can produce.

    Apply

    For each situation, decide whether it is binomial. When it is, identify n, p, and the requested x, then calculate and interpret.

    1. A player makes each free throw with probability 0.7. For 6 independent free throws, find the probability of exactly 4 makes and interpret it.
      • This is binomial: fixed n = 6, two outcomes, independent attempts, constant p = 0.7. For x = 4, P(4) = 6C4(0.7)4(0.3)2 = 0.3241. Under this model, there is about a 32.4% chance the player makes exactly 4 of these 6 free throws.
    2. A seed has a 0.2 chance of sprouting. If 5 seeds sprout independently, find the probability exactly 2 sprout.
      • This is binomial with n = 5, x = 2, and p = 0.2. P(2) = 5C2(0.2)2(0.8)3 = 10(0.04)(0.512) = 0.2048. About 20.48% of groups of 5 would have exactly 2 sprouts.
    3. A player makes 50% of free throws. Over 10 independent attempts, what is the expected number of makes? Interpret it.
      • n = 10 and p = 0.5, so np = 10(0.5) = 5 makes on average over many sets of 10 attempts. One set need not have exactly 5.
    4. You draw 5 cards without replacement from a standard deck and count the hearts. Is this binomial? Explain which condition fails.
      • This is not binomial. Draws without replacement are dependent and the probability of a heart changes each time.

    Closure

    How is a binomial calculation similar to the simulations we used earlier to understand statistical significance?

    Explain in your own words what is held constant, what outcomes are counted, and what an unusual result suggests.

    • Both start with a chance model, such as a null-hypothesis success rate, and ask what results could happen if it were true. Simulation estimates how often results occur by repeating the process; a binomial calculation finds those probabilities directly. A very small probability marks a result as unusual under the model, not impossible.

    Lesson 21

    Introduction to the Normal Distribution & Empirical Rule

    Big Idea

    Many quantitative measurements have a roughly symmetric, bell-shaped distribution, and the normal model gives us a predictable way to describe how values are distributed.

    Today we will describe a distribution using its center and spread, then estimate how much data lie near the mean.

    Learning Goals

    • Recognize a distribution that is approximately bell-shaped and symmetric.
    • Explain how the mean describes center and standard deviation describes spread.
    • Use the Empirical Rule (68–95–99.7 rule) to estimate percentages and values.
    • Recognize that the rule is an approximation for distributions that are reasonably normal.

    Explore: What Does a Normal Distribution Look Like?

    Compare these simple distribution sketches. Which one looks approximately normal?

    1. Which sketch is most symmetric and bell-shaped?
      • Distribution A is approximately symmetric with one central peak, so it is the best match for a normal shape.
    2. Where is the center of Distribution A, and how could you describe its spread?
      • The center is near the middle of the horizontal axis, where the peak is. Spread describes how broadly values fan out around that center.
    3. Which values might appear unusual in an approximately normal distribution?
      • Values far out in either tail, several standard deviations from the mean, are less common and may appear unusual.

    For a normal model, the mean marks the center. The standard deviation describes the spread: a larger standard deviation makes the curve wider, while a smaller one makes it narrower.

    The Empirical Rule

    For a distribution that is reasonably normal, these approximate percentages fall within each distance from the mean:

    68%
    within 1 standard deviation

    95%
    within 2 standard deviations

    99.7%
    within 3 standard deviations

    Outside those intervals: about 32% outside ±1 SD, 5% outside ±2 SD, and 0.3% outside ±3 SD.

    Outside percentages combine both tails. If the distribution is symmetric, about half of each outside percentage lies in either tail.

    Approximate does not mean exact. Use the Empirical Rule only when the distribution is reasonably bell-shaped and symmetric. It is not a rule for every data set.

    Hand Calculation Practice

    Guiding Questions

    1. What shape and symmetry describe a distribution that is approximately normal?
      • It is roughly bell-shaped, with one central peak and sides that are approximately mirror images.
    2. Where is the center of a normal distribution?
      • The mean is at the center of the normal distribution.
    3. What does the standard deviation tell us about the distribution?
      • It describes the spread of values around the mean; a larger standard deviation means a wider distribution.
    4. What does the 68% in the Empirical Rule mean?
      • About 68% of values in a reasonably normal distribution fall between one standard deviation below and one above the mean.
    5. What does the 95% mean?
      • About 95% of values fall within two standard deviations of the mean.
    6. What does the 99.7% mean?
      • About 99.7% of values fall within three standard deviations of the mean.
    7. How do you find values one, two, or three standard deviations from the mean?
      • Multiply the standard deviation by 1, 2, or 3, then add that amount to the mean for values above it or subtract it for values below it.
    8. Why is the Empirical Rule only an approximation?
      • Real distributions are not perfectly normal, and the rule describes approximate percentages only for distributions that are reasonably bell-shaped and symmetric.

    The Big Idea

    A normal distribution is approximately symmetric and bell-shaped. The mean tells us where the distribution is centered, while the standard deviation tells us how spread out it is. The Empirical Rule lets us estimate how much data fall within 1, 2, and 3 standard deviations of the mean.

    The rule is useful when the distribution is reasonably normal, but it is not exact and should not be applied to strongly skewed or irregular data.

    Apply

    Use the Empirical Rule only for the contexts described as reasonably normal. A value more than 2 standard deviations from the mean is relatively unusual; beyond 3 standard deviations is very unusual.

    1. Adult heights in a group are reasonably normal with mean 170 cm and standard deviation 7 cm. Find the values 1, 2, and 3 standard deviations from the mean, then estimate the percentage between 156 cm and 184 cm.
      • The 1-SD values are 163 and 177 cm; 2-SD values are 156 and 184 cm; 3-SD values are 149 and 191 cm. The interval 156–184 cm is within 2 SD, so it contains about 95% of the heights.
    2. Test scores are reasonably normal with mean 75 points and standard deviation 8 points. Estimate the percentage between 67 and 83, and decide whether a score of 99 is unusual.
      • 67 and 83 are 1 SD from the mean, so about 68% of scores fall in that interval. A score of 99 is 3 SD above the mean (75 + 3 × 8), so it is very unusual under this model.
    3. Measurement errors are reasonably normal with mean 0 mm and standard deviation 2 mm. Estimate the percentage of errors between −4 mm and 4 mm, and the percentage greater than 4 mm.
      • −4 and 4 are 2 SD from the mean, so about 95% of errors are in that interval. About 5% is outside both ends, so approximately 2.5% is greater than 4 mm.
    4. A machine's fill amounts are reasonably normal with mean 500 mL and standard deviation 10 mL. Find the 3-SD interval and identify whether 525 mL is unusual.
      • The 3-SD interval is 500 ± 30, or 470 to 530 mL. A fill of 525 mL is 2.5 SD above the mean, so it is uncommon and lies beyond the 2-SD interval, though still within 3 SD.
    5. Household incomes in a region have many values clustered at the low end and a long tail toward very high incomes. Should the Empirical Rule be used? Explain.
      • No. This distribution is strongly right-skewed rather than approximately symmetric and bell-shaped, so the Empirical Rule percentages are not appropriate.

    Closure

    If you know the mean and standard deviation of a reasonably normal distribution, what can you predict about where most of the data will fall?

    • You can estimate that about 68% of values fall within 1 standard deviation, 95% within 2, and 99.7% within 3 standard deviations of the mean.

    Lesson 22

    Normal Distribution & Z-Scores

    Big Idea

    A z-score standardizes a value by telling us how many standard deviations it is above or below the mean.

    Once a value is expressed as a z-score, a z-table lets us find the proportion of a normal distribution below, above, or between values.

    Learning Goals

    • Calculate and interpret positive, negative, and zero z-scores.
    • Use a printed z-table to find proportions and convert them to percentages or percentiles.
    • Find probabilities above and between values, and work backward from a percentile to a z-score.
    • Use StatLab to verify, not replace, hand calculations.

    Explore: Standardizing a Score

    A test has a mean of 75 and a standard deviation of 10. Jordan scores 87.

    z = (x − μ) / σ = (87 − 75) / 10 = 12 / 10 = 1.2

    The score is 1.2 standard deviations above the mean. Notice that z is a location measured in standard deviations, not another test score.

    1. Explain what z = 1.2 means for Jordan's score.
      • Jordan's score is 1.2 standard deviations above the class mean of 75.
    2. Use your printed z-table to find the area to the left of z = 1.20. Convert it to a percentile.
      • The left-tail area is about 0.8849. As a percentage, that is 88.49%, so Jordan is around the 88th percentile in this normal model.

    Complete process: value → z-score → z-table → probability or percentile

    The Z-Score Formula

    z = (x − μ) / σ

    • x = individual value
    • μ = population mean
    • σ = population standard deviation
    • z = number of standard deviations the value is from the mean

    Hand Calculation Practice

    Use your printed z-table. Answers may be rounded to four decimal places or the nearest hundredth of a percent.

    1. A class has mean score 80 and standard deviation 5. Find the z-score for a score of 74 and interpret its sign.
      • z = (74 − 80) / 5 = −1.2. The score is 1.2 standard deviations below the mean.
    2. For the standard normal model, use the table to find the proportion below z = 0.50. Convert it to a percentage and percentile.
      • The area to the left is about 0.6915 = 69.15%. This is about the 69th percentile.
    3. For a normal model, what proportion is above z = 1.00?
      • The table gives about 0.8413 to the left. Above is 1 − 0.8413 = 0.1587, or 15.87%.
    4. Find the proportion between z = −1.00 and z = 1.00.
      • Area between = area left of 1.00 minus area left of −1.00 = 0.8413 − 0.1587 = 0.6826, or 68.26%.
    5. A running time is at the 90th percentile in a normal model. Use the z-table to estimate its z-score.
      • Look for an area near 0.9000 in the table. The corresponding z-score is about 1.28, meaning 1.28 standard deviations above the mean.
    6. Adult heights are modeled with mean 170 cm and standard deviation 6 cm. Find the z-score for a height of 158 cm, then estimate the proportion below that height.
      • z = (158 − 170) / 6 = −2. The table gives an area to the left of about 0.0228, or 2.28%.
    7. Manufacturing measurements have mean 50 mm and standard deviation 2 mm. Find the proportion between 48 mm and 52 mm.
      • The endpoints have z-scores −1 and +1. Subtract table areas: 0.8413 − 0.1587 = 0.6826, or 68.26%.

    Guiding Questions

    1. What does a z-score measure?
      • It measures how many standard deviations a value is above or below the mean.
    2. What does a positive z-score mean?
      • The value is above the mean.
    3. What does a negative z-score mean?
      • The value is below the mean.
    4. What does z = 0 mean?
      • The value is exactly at the mean, so it is zero standard deviations from the mean.
    5. How do you calculate a z-score from a value, mean, and standard deviation?
      • Subtract the mean from the value, then divide that difference by the standard deviation: z = (x − μ) / σ.
    6. How does a z-table help find probabilities?
      • After converting a value to a z-score, the table gives the area or proportion to the left of that z-score for a normal model.
    7. How do you find the probability above a value when your table gives area to the left?
      • Subtract the left-tail area from 1: area above = 1 − area below.
    8. How do you find the probability between two values?
      • Find a z-score and left-tail area for each endpoint, then subtract the smaller left-tail area from the larger one.

    The Big Idea

    A z-score puts a value on a common scale by measuring how many standard deviations it is from the mean. Once a value is standardized, a z-table can be used to determine probabilities and percentiles.

    A z-score keeps the value's relative location while removing the original measurement scale. The sign tells direction, and the size tells distance in standard-deviation units.

    Apply

    Show the z-score and z-table work by hand first. Then check your probability in StatLab.

    1. Test scores are normal with μ = 500 and σ = 100. Find and interpret the z-score for a score of 650.
      • z = (650 − 500) / 100 = 1.50. The score is 1.5 standard deviations above the mean.
    2. Adult heights are modeled with μ = 170 cm and σ = 6 cm. Find and interpret the z-score for a height of 161 cm.
      • z = (161 − 170) / 6 = −1.50. The height is 1.5 standard deviations below the mean.
    3. Running times are normal with μ = 20 minutes and σ = 2 minutes. What proportion of times are below 18 minutes?
      • z = (18 − 20) / 2 = −1.00. The area to the left is about 0.1587, or 15.87%.
    4. Manufacturing measurements are normal with μ = 50 mm and σ = 2 mm. What proportion is above 53 mm?
      • z = (53 − 50) / 2 = 1.50. Area above = 1 − 0.9332 = 0.0668, or 6.68%.
    5. SAT-style scores are normal with μ = 1000 and σ = 100. Estimate the proportion between 900 and 1100.
      • The endpoints have z-scores −1 and +1. Subtract the left areas: 0.8413 − 0.1587 = 0.6826, or 68.26%.
    6. A score has z = 1.28. Use the z-table to estimate its percentile and explain the meaning.
      • The area to the left of z = 1.28 is about 0.8997, or approximately the 90th percentile. About 90% of values are at or below this score in the model.
    7. For a normal model with μ = 500 and σ = 20, find the raw value at the 90th percentile. Start with the z-table.
      • The table's area near 0.9000 corresponds to z ≈ 1.28. Solve x = μ + zσ = 500 + 1.28(20) = 525.6. The raw value is about 525.6 units.

    Closure

    Why is a z-score more useful than simply knowing whether a score is above or below the mean?

    • A z-score tells both the direction and how far the value is from the mean in standard-deviation units. That lets us compare values from different scales and use the z-table to estimate probabilities and percentiles.

    Lesson 23

    Confidence Intervals for Proportions & Sample Size

    Big Idea

    We can use a sample proportion to estimate a population proportion while also quantifying the uncertainty in that estimate.

    A survey gives us a proportion from a sample. A confidence interval uses that sample result to estimate a range of plausible values for the population proportion.

    Learning Goals

    • Calculate a sample proportion, p̂, and distinguish it from a population proportion, p.
    • Identify a point estimate and calculate a margin of error.
    • Construct and interpret a confidence interval for a population proportion.
    • Explain how sample size affects margin of error.
    • Calculate a required sample size for a desired margin of error.

    Part 1: Sample Proportions

    p̂ = xn

    • x = number of successes, or sample members with the characteristic being counted
    • n = sample size
    • p̂ = sample proportion, the fraction of the sample that had the characteristic; q̂ = 1 − p̂ is the sample proportion without it, and q = 1 − p is the population proportion without it

    Example: 72 of 120 students surveyed prefer a later school start time. Here x = 72 and n = 120, so p̂ = 72120 = 0.60, or 60%.

    The sample proportion p̂ is a point estimate: one best single-number estimate, based on this sample, for the unknown population proportion p.

    1. In the later-start survey, identify x and n, then calculate p̂ as a percent.
      • x = 72 and n = 120. p̂ = 72120 = 0.60, so 60% of the sampled students prefer a later start.

    Part 2: Confidence Interval for a Proportion

    p̂ ± z* √p̂q̂n

    • p̂ is the center of the interval and the sample estimate.
    • z* is the confidence-level multiplier. For 90%, 95%, and 99% confidence, use approximately 1.645, 1.96, and 2.576.
    • √p̂q̂n is the standard error.
    • Margin of error (ME) is the entire amount added to and subtracted from p̂: z* times the standard error.

    Process: sample data → p̂ → margin of error → confidence interval → interpretation

    Build a 95% confidence interval

    For the 72 of 120 students, p̂ = 0.60, n = 120, and z* = 1.96.

    Standard error: √0.60(0.40)120 = √0.002 ≈ 0.0447

    Margin of error: ME = 1.96(0.0447) ≈ 0.0876, or 8.8 percentage points

    Confidence interval: 0.60 ± 0.0876 = (0.512, 0.688), or about 51.2% to 68.8%

    Interpretation: We are 95% confident that the proportion of all students who prefer a later start time is between about 51.2% and 68.8%. The 95% describes the long-run success rate of this interval method across repeated random samples; it does not mean that 95% of students are in the interval.

    Part 3: Hand Calculation Practice

    Use z* = 1.96 for each 95% interval. Show p̂, identify n, calculate ME, write both endpoints, and interpret the interval in context. Round proportions to three decimals.

      • p̂ = 4575 = 0.60, q̂ = 0.40, and n = 75. ME = 1.96√0.60(0.40)75 ≈ 0.111. The interval is 0.60 ± 0.111 = (0.489, 0.711). We are 95% confident that 48.9% to 71.1% of all students support the later start.

    Part 4: Sample Size

    Larger sample → smaller margin of error

    For a chosen confidence level and target margin of error, estimate the required sample size with:

    n = (z*)² p*q*ME²

    • ME = desired margin of error, written as a decimal
    • p* = prior estimate of the population proportion; q* = 1 − p*
    • z* = critical value for the desired confidence level

    If no prior estimate for p is available, use p* = 0.50, so q* = 0.50. Since p*q* is largest at 0.50, this choice gives the most conservative, or largest, required sample size. Always round n up to ensure the desired margin of error is met.

    Sample Size Practice

    Substitute into the formula, then round each result up to a whole person.

    2. Find the required sample size for 90% confidence, ME = 0.04, and no prior estimate for p. Use z* = 1.645.
      • Use p* = 0.50 and q* = 0.50: n = (1.645)²(0.50)(0.50)(0.04)² ≈ 422.82. Round up to 423 people.
    3. A prior study estimates p = 0.60. Find the required sample size for 95% confidence and ME = 0.05.

    4. There is no prior estimate for p. Find the sample size for 99% confidence and ME = 0.04. Use z* = 2.576.

    Guiding Questions

    1. What is p̂?
      • p̂ is the sample proportion: the number of sampled successes divided by the sample size.
    2. How is a sample proportion different from a population proportion?
      • p̂ describes the observed sample; p describes the usually unknown proportion in the whole population.
    3. What is a point estimate?
      • It is one sample-based number used as an estimate of a population parameter; here p̂ estimates p.
    4. What does a confidence interval estimate?
      • It estimates a range of plausible values for the population proportion p.
    5. What is the margin of error?
      • It is the amount added to and subtracted from p̂ to form the interval; it reflects the estimate's sampling uncertainty at the chosen confidence level.
    6. What generally happens to margin of error when sample size increases?
      • It gets smaller, so the interval is narrower and the estimate is more precise.
    7. Why use p* = 0.50 when no prior estimate is available?
      • For a proportion, p*q* is largest at 0.50, so this choice gives the largest, most conservative required sample size.
    8. Why must a calculated sample size be rounded up?
      • Rounding down could leave too few observations and make the actual margin of error slightly larger than the target.

    Apply

    Show your calculations by hand, then use technology to check. State the confidence level and interpret intervals in context.

      • p̂ = 4270 = 0.60, or 60%, and n = 70.

      • ME = 1.645√0.60(0.40)70 ≈ 0.096. The interval is 0.60 ± 0.096 = (0.504, 0.696). We are 90% confident that 50.4% to 69.6% of all students support the policy.

      • For 90%, ME = 1.645√0.25100 ≈ 0.082, giving (0.418, 0.582). For 99%, ME = 2.576√0.25100 = 0.129, giving (0.371, 0.629). The 99% interval is wider because greater confidence needs a larger multiplier.
    4. For p̂ = 0.60 and 95% confidence, compare the margin of error for n = 100 with n = 400. How does precision change?
      • At n = 100, ME = 1.96√0.60(0.40)100 ≈ 0.096. At n = 400, ME ≈ 0.048. Quadrupling n halves the margin of error, so the estimate is more precise.
    5. A community group wants 95% confidence with ME = 0.03 and has no prior estimate for p. Find the required sample size and explain your rounding.
      • Use z* = 1.96, p* = 0.50, and q* = 0.50: n = (1.96)²(0.25)(0.03)² ≈ 1,067.11. Round up to 1,068 people so the target margin of error is met.

      • Raising confidence increases z*, so it requires a larger sample if ME stays fixed. Lowering ME also increases required n because ME is squared in the denominator; for example, changing 0.05 to 0.03 multiplies n by 0.050.03² ≈ 2.78. Which change requires more depends on the exact comparison; with 95% to 99%, z* changes from 1.96 to 2.576, multiplying n by about 1.73, so reducing ME from 0.05 to 0.03 requires the larger sample.

      • We are 95% confident that between 62% and 78% of all customers are satisfied with the product.

    The Big Idea

    A sample proportion gives us an estimate of a population proportion. A confidence interval adds a measure of uncertainty to that estimate. Larger samples generally produce more precise estimates, while higher confidence requires a wider interval or a larger sample.

    Closure

    1. How does a confidence interval improve on reporting only a sample proportion?
      • It reports the sample estimate along with a range of plausible population proportions and a measure of the estimate's uncertainty.
    2. Why does increasing the sample size usually make a confidence interval narrower?
      • A larger n makes the standard error √p̂q̂n smaller, which usually lowers the margin of error and narrows the interval.

    Lesson 24

    Confidence Intervals with the t-Distribution & Chi-Squared

    Big Idea

    Different types of data and unknown population information require different statistical models.

    Today we will estimate a population mean when its standard deviation is unknown, then compare categorical counts with what a model predicts.

    Learning Goals

    • Choose a t-distribution when estimating a population mean and σ is unknown.
    • Distinguish population standard deviation σ from sample standard deviation s.
    • Calculate degrees of freedom, find a t-critical value, and construct and interpret a confidence interval for a population mean.
    • Find expected categorical counts and calculate contributions to a chi-squared statistic.
    • Determine degrees of freedom and use a table or technology to interpret a chi-squared goodness-of-fit result.

    Part A: Confidence Intervals with the t-Distribution

    Use z when the population standard deviation is known; use t when it is estimated from the sample.

    When σ is unknown, we use the sample standard deviation s to estimate the population spread. The t-distribution accounts for the additional uncertainty created by estimating σ from the sample.

    x̄ ± t*s√n

    • x̄ = sample mean
    • s = sample standard deviation
    • n = sample size
    • t* = t-critical value for the confidence level and degrees of freedom
    • Degrees of freedom: df = n − 1
    • Margin of error: ME = t* times the standard error, s√n
    Choose z or t
    Choosing a confidence-interval model for a mean
    MethodPopulation SDSpread usedModel
    zσ knownPopulation SD σNormal distribution
    tσ unknownSample SD st-distribution accounts for extra uncertainty
    Guided Example: Student Sleep

    A sample of 9 students reported these hours of sleep on a school night: 6, 7, 8, 7, 6, 9, 7, 8, 6. The sample mean is x̄ = 649 ≈ 7.11 hours and the sample standard deviation is s ≈ 1.05 hours. The population standard deviation σ is unknown.

    For this guided calculation, use a t-table entry for a 95% confidence interval and df = 8: t* = 2.306.

    Sample size: n = 9, so df = n − 1 = 8.

    Standard error: s√n = 1.05√9 = 0.35 hours.

    Margin of error: ME = 2.306(0.35) ≈ 0.81 hours.

    Confidence interval: 7.11 ± 0.81 = (6.30, 7.92) hours.

    Interpretation: We are 95% confident that the mean amount of sleep for the population represented by these students is between about 6.30 and 7.92 hours per school night.

    1. Why is t appropriate for the sleep example, and what are n and df?
      • σ is unknown, so the sample standard deviation s is used and the t-distribution accounts for the extra uncertainty. n = 9 and df = 9 − 1 = 8.
    2. Using t* = 2.306, calculate the margin of error and state the interval's meaning in context.
      • ME = 2.306(1.05 / √9) = 2.306(0.35) ≈ 0.81 hours. The 95% interval is (7.11 − 0.81, 7.11 + 0.81) = (6.30, 7.92); we are 95% confident the population mean sleep time is in this range.

      • Use a t-distribution because the population standard deviation σ is unknown and s = 3 cm estimates it. df = 21 − 1 = 20.

    5. A report gives x̄ = 7.5 hours, s = 1.2 hours, n = 10, and a 95% interval (6.64, 8.36). Interpret this interval for the population mean.
      • We are 95% confident that the population mean is between 6.64 and 8.36 hours. The interval estimates the mean, not the range containing 95% of individual observations.

    Part B: Chi-Squared Goodness-of-Fit

    Chi-squared methods compare observed categorical data with what we would expect under a particular model.

    In a goodness-of-fit test, we compare the count observed in each category with the count predicted by a model.

    χ² = Σ (O − E)²E

    • O = observed count in a category
    • E = expected count in that category under the model
    • Σ = add all category contributions
    • Degrees of freedom: df = number of categories − 1
    Guided Example: Is the Die Fair?

    A die is rolled 60 times. If the die is fair, each face is expected to appear equally often, so the expected count is 60 / 6 = 10 per face.

    Observed and expected die outcomes
    Face123456
    Observed (O)812911713
    Expected (E)101010101010

    For face 1, O = 8 and E = 10, so its contribution is (8 − 10)²10 = 0.4. Calculate the remaining category contributions, then add them:

    Face 2: (12 − 10)²10 = 0.4; face 3: (9 − 10)²10 = 0.1

    Face 4: (11 − 10)²10 = 0.1; face 5: (7 − 10)²10 = 0.9; face 6: (13 − 10)²10 = 0.9

    χ² = 0.4 + 0.4 + 0.1 + 0.1 + 0.9 + 0.9 = 2.8

    df = 6 − 1 = 5. For df = 5, a chi-squared table gives a 0.05 upper-tail critical value of about 11.07. Since 2.8 is smaller, the result is not unusually large (technology gives p ≈ 0.73).

    Interpretation: These observed counts are reasonably consistent with the fair-die model. A small χ² means the observed counts are relatively close to expected counts; a large χ² means larger differences and may be evidence against the model. It does not prove the die is fair.

    1. Find the expected number of times each face should appear in 60 rolls of a fair die.
      • There are 6 equally likely faces, so the expected count is 60 / 6 = 10 rolls per face.
    2. Calculate the contribution for face 1, then state df and the total χ² for the six observed counts.
      • Face 1 contributes (8 − 10)² / 10 = 0.4. With 6 categories, df = 6 − 1 = 5; adding all six contributions gives χ² = 2.8.
    3. Using the df = 5 critical value 11.07 at the 0.05 level, what does χ² = 2.8 suggest about the fair-die model?
      • Because 2.8 is below 11.07 (and the technology p-value is about 0.73), the counts are not unusually far from what a fair die predicts; they are reasonably consistent with the model.

    For larger tests, use a calculator or statistical software to compute χ² and the p-value. The statistic summarizes the size of the observed-versus-expected differences; the p-value helps decide whether that amount of difference is unusual under the model.

    Chi-Squared Practice

    Use a table or technology for the final inference when needed. Keep each category contribution separate before adding.

      • Each section has probability 1 / 4, so its expected count is 40(1 / 4) = 10 spins.
    2. For one category, O = 14 and E = 10. Calculate its chi-squared contribution.
      • Contribution = (14 − 10)²10 = 16 / 10 = 1.6.
    3. A three-category model has observed counts 18, 22, and 20; each expected count is 20. Find each contribution and the total χ².
      • The contributions are (18 − 20)² / 20 = 0.2, (22 − 20)² / 20 = 0.2, and (20 − 20)² / 20 = 0. The total is χ² = 0.4.
    4. How many degrees of freedom are used for a goodness-of-fit test with 3 categories?
      • df = number of categories − 1 = 3 − 1 = 2.
    5. A goodness-of-fit test has df = 2 and χ² = 8.0. A table gives the 0.05 upper-tail critical value 5.99. What does the comparison suggest?
      • Since 8.0 is greater than 5.99, the statistic is in the upper 5% tail under the model. The observed counts differ more than expected by chance alone at this cutoff, providing evidence against the model.

    Guiding Questions

    1. When should we use t instead of z for a confidence interval for a mean?
      • Use t when the population standard deviation σ is unknown and is estimated with the sample standard deviation s.
    2. Why is s used when σ is unknown?
      • s is calculated from the sample and estimates the unknown population spread; using an estimate adds uncertainty that t accounts for.
    3. What are degrees of freedom for a one-sample t interval?
      • They are df = n − 1, the value used to select the appropriate t-distribution and critical value.
    4. What does the t-critical value t* represent?
      • It is the multiplier, selected by confidence level and df, that sets how many standard errors extend on each side of the sample mean.
    5. What does a confidence interval using t estimate?
      • It estimates a plausible range for the population mean μ.
    6. In a goodness-of-fit test, what do observed and expected counts mean?
      • Observed counts are the data collected in each category; expected counts are the counts predicted by the model being checked.
    7. What does the chi-squared statistic measure?
      • It adds up the squared observed-minus-expected differences, scaled by expected counts, to summarize how far the data are from the model.
    8. What does a large chi-squared statistic suggest?
      • It suggests the observed counts are far from expected counts; a sufficiently large value may be evidence against the model.

    Apply

    Show the model choice and setup. Use a t-table, chi-squared table, or technology for critical values and final inference.

    1. A random sample of 12 runners has mean mile time x̄ = 8.4 minutes and sample SD s = 0.6 minutes. The population SD is unknown. Explain why t, not z, is appropriate and find df.
      • σ is unknown, and s = 0.6 minutes estimates it, so use t to account for that extra uncertainty. df = 12 − 1 = 11.
    2. For the runners, use t* = 2.201 for a 95% interval with df = 11. Find ME and construct the interval for mean mile time.

    4. A fair four-color spinner is spun 80 times. Find the expected count for each color, then calculate the contribution for a color observed 16 times.

      • Since p = 0.37 is greater than 0.05, the observed differences are not statistically unusual under the model. We do not have sufficient evidence to conclude that the categorical distribution differs from the expected model.

    The Big Idea

    The statistical method should match the type of data and the information we know about the population.

    For t: When estimating a population mean and the population standard deviation is unknown, the t-distribution accounts for the extra uncertainty.

    For chi-squared: Chi-squared measures how different the observed categorical counts are from the counts we would expect under a model.

    Closure

    1. Why can't we use exactly the same statistical method for every type of data?
      • Different questions use different kinds of data and population information. A t interval estimates a mean using sample spread when σ is unknown, while chi-squared compares categorical counts with expected counts from a model.
    2. How is a t-based confidence interval different from a chi-squared analysis?
      • A t-based confidence interval uses a sample mean and sample standard deviation to estimate a population mean; a chi-squared analysis uses observed and expected categorical counts to assess their difference from a model.

    Lesson 25

    Hypothesis Testing

    Big Idea

    Hypothesis testing provides a structured way to determine whether sample evidence is strong enough to reject a null hypothesis.

    We will turn a sample result into a probability, compare that probability with a chosen cutoff, and make a conclusion about a claim.

    Learning Goals

    • State a null hypothesis and an alternative hypothesis.
    • Identify a significance level, α, and distinguish one-sided from two-sided tests.
    • Calculate a test statistic and find and interpret a p-value.
    • Compare a p-value with α, decide whether to reject or fail to reject H₀, and conclude in context.
    • Connect hypothesis testing to simulation, binomial probability, and normal distributions.

    Part 1: The Hypothesis Testing Process

    Hypothesis testing is a structured version of the inference process we have been using throughout the course.

    1. 1. State H₀ and Hₐ
    2. 2. Identify α
    3. 3. Calculate the test statistic
    4. 4. Find the p-value
    5. 5. Compare p-value with α
    6. 6. Decide
    7. 7. Conclude in context
    Write the hypotheses
    • H₀, the null hypothesis: the starting claim or “no effect/no difference” model. It includes an equality.
    • Hₐ, the alternative hypothesis: the direction or difference we are looking for evidence to support.
    Alternative hypotheses for a population proportion
    QuestionAlternativeTest direction
    Has the percentage changed?Hₐ: p ≠ p₀Two-sided
    Has the percentage increased?Hₐ: p > p₀Right-sided
    Has the percentage decreased?Hₐ: p < p₀Left-sided

    Choose the alternative from the research question before looking at the sample result. The direction tells us which outcomes count as evidence against H₀.

    1. A company asks whether customer preference has changed from 50%. Write the alternative hypothesis and identify the test direction.
      • Hₐ: p ≠ 0.50. The question allows a change in either direction, so this is a two-sided test.
    2. A school asks whether support for a policy has increased above 40%. Write Hₐ and identify the direction.
      • Hₐ: p > 0.40. The question looks for an increase, so this is a right-sided test.
    3. A survey asks whether customer satisfaction has decreased below 80%. Write Hₐ and identify the direction.
      • Hₐ: p < 0.80. The question looks for a decrease, so this is a left-sided test.

    Part 2: Significance Level and Decision

    A common significance level is α = 0.05. Alpha is the cutoff for deciding whether the sample result would be sufficiently unusual if H₀ were true.

    If p-value ≤ α: Reject H₀

    If p-value > α: Fail to reject H₀

    Fail to reject does not mean “prove H₀ is true.” It means the sample did not provide strong enough evidence against H₀ at the chosen significance level.

    p-value: Assuming H₀ is true, the probability of getting a result at least as extreme as the one observed, in the direction specified by Hₐ.

      • Hₐ: p ≠ 0.45. “Changed” includes an increase or decrease, so this is two-sided.

    Common Mistakes

    • “The p-value is the probability that H₀ is true.” Incorrect. It is a probability of data this extreme or more extreme, assuming H₀ is true.
    • “Fail to reject means H₀ is proven true.” Incorrect. It means the evidence was not strong enough to reject H₀ at the chosen α.
    • “Statistically significant means practically important.” Not necessarily. Statistical significance does not by itself tell us whether an effect is large or meaningful in practice.
    • Choosing Hₐ after seeing the data. Incorrect. Choose the alternative from the research question before examining the sample result.
    • Forgetting to state the conclusion in context. Connect the decision to the original claim and population.
    • Confusing one-sided and two-sided tests. Match the tail or tails to the wording of Hₐ.

    Guiding Questions

    1. What is the purpose of a hypothesis test?
      • It provides a structured way to decide whether sample evidence is strong enough to reject a stated null hypothesis.
    2. What does H₀ represent?
      • H₀ is the null model or starting claim, often representing no effect or no difference.
    3. What does Hₐ represent?
      • Hₐ is the alternative claim we are looking for evidence to support, such as a difference, increase, or decrease.
    4. What does α represent?
      • α is the significance-level cutoff used to decide whether the p-value is small enough to reject H₀.
    5. How do you decide whether a test is one-sided or two-sided?
      • Use the research question: “different” uses two sides, “increased” uses the right side, and “decreased” uses the left side. Decide before looking at the data.
    6. What does the test statistic measure?
      • It measures how far the sample result is from the null value in standard-error units.
    7. What does the p-value tell us?
      • Assuming H₀ is true, it gives the probability of getting the observed result or one at least as extreme in the direction specified by Hₐ.
    8. Why does a small p-value provide evidence against H₀?
      • A small p-value means the observed result would be unusual if H₀ were true, so the null model may not explain the data well.

    The Big Idea

    Hypothesis testing gives us a formal process for deciding whether sample evidence is strong enough to reject a null hypothesis. The p-value measures how unusual the observed result would be if the null hypothesis were true.

    Statistics starts with variability and uncertainty. We collect data, describe the data, model chance, quantify uncertainty, and use evidence to make informed conclusions.

    Closure

    1. How is a formal hypothesis test different from simply looking at two numbers and deciding that one is bigger?
      • A formal test accounts for sample variability, specifies hypotheses and a significance level, measures how unusual the result is under H₀, and uses that evidence to make a careful conclusion in context.
    2. Explain the connection among simulation, p-values, and hypothesis testing.
      • Simulation estimates how often extreme results occur under the null model; a p-value gives that probability using a model or calculation; hypothesis testing compares it with α to make a formal decision. The calculations are tools for answering the larger statistical question, not the goal by themselves.
