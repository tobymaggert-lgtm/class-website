Lesson 9

What Does "Statistically Significant" Mean?

Bellringer

Is this result surprising?

A basketball player normally makes about 50% of their free throws. In one game, they make 9 out of 10.

  • Is 9 out of 10 surprising?
  • Could this happen just by chance?
  • Does this prove the player has become a better free-throw shooter?
  • How could we investigate whether a result like this is unusual?
  • 9 out of 10 is higher than the player's usual rate, so it may seem surprising.
  • It could still happen by chance, especially in a small sample.
  • It does not prove the player has improved.
  • We could simulate many sets of 10 free throws with a 50% success rate and compare the simulated results with 9 out of 10.

Objectives

  • Explain the idea of a null hypothesisAn informal assumption that nothing unusual or changed is happening..
  • Distinguish an observed result from results produced by chance.
  • Use simulation to investigate whether an observed result is surprising.
  • Explain statistical significanceEvidence that an observed result would be unusual if the null hypothesis were true. conceptually.
  • Explain why an unusual result does not automatically prove that something caused it.

Explore: What Could Chance Produce?

Start with the assumption that the player's true free-throw probability is still 50%.

This is our informal null hypothesisThe assumption that nothing unusual is happening or that the player's true success rate is still 50%.: nothing has changed.

If we repeatedly simulate 10 free throws with a 50% success rate, the number of makes will vary from simulation to simulation.

  • What number of makes would you expect?
  • Would 7 out of 10 be unusual?
  • What about 9 out of 10?
  • What about 10 out of 10?
  • How can we determine how unusual 9 out of 10 really is?
  • We would expect results near 5 makes, although other results can occur.
  • Seven out of 10 is possible and may not be especially unusual.
  • Nine out of 10 and 10 out of 10 should occur less often.
  • We can determine how unusual 9 out of 10 is by simulating many groups of 10 free throws under the 50% assumption and counting how often the simulations produce 9 or more makes.

    Guiding Questions

    • What is the observed result?

      The observed result is the result we actually saw, such as 9 makes in 10 free throws.

    • What does the null hypothesis assume?

      It assumes that nothing unusual has changed. In this investigation, the player's true free-throw probability is still 50%.

    • Why do we need a null hypothesis before running the simulation?

      It gives us a chance-based model to use as a comparison for deciding whether the observed result is unusual.

    • What does each simulation represent?

      Each simulation represents one possible result if the null hypothesis were true.

    • Why do we run many simulations?

      Many simulations show the range and frequency of results that chance alone can produce.

    • What does it mean for a result to be surprising?

      A surprising result occurs rarely in simulations conducted under the null hypothesis.

    • Is an unusual result impossible under the null hypothesis?

      No. An unusual result can still happen by chance; it is just less common.

    • If an observed result is very unusual under the null hypothesis, what might that suggest?

      It may suggest that the null hypothesis does not explain the observed result very well and that something may have changed.

    • Does statistical significance prove that the null hypothesis is false?

      No. Statistical significance is evidence against the null hypothesis, not absolute proof that it is false.

    • Why should we consider the amount of data when deciding whether a result is convincing?

      Larger samples usually have less random variability, so an unusual result in a larger sample can be more convincing.

    • How is this investigation connected to Lesson 3?

      Both lessons use simulation to investigate what results are possible when a chance process or null model is assumed.

    • Why is "could this happen by chance?" such an important statistical question?

      It helps us separate ordinary random variation from results that provide evidence that something unusual may be happening.

    The Big Idea

    We compare observed results to what chance alone might produce.

    Statistical significanceEvidence that an observed result would be unusual if the null hypothesis were true. is about how unusual an observed result would be if the null hypothesis were true. Randomness naturally produces variability, so even a fair process can sometimes produce an extreme result.

    We use simulation to see what chance alone can produce. Common results are not surprising. Very unusual results provide evidence against the null hypothesis, but statistical significance is about evidence, not absolute proof. We do not yet calculate formal p-values; we first build the idea by comparing the observed result with simulated results.

    The reasoning pattern is:

    Observed result → Assume nothing unusual → Simulate chance → Compare → Decide how surprising the result is

    Vocabulary:

    null hypothesisThe assumption that nothing unusual or changed is happening., observed resultThe result actually seen in the data., simulationA model or repeated random process used to investigate what results chance can produce., and statistical significanceEvidence that a result is unusual under the null hypothesis..

    Apply

    For each scenario, state the null hypothesis, identify the observed result, explain how simulation could be used, and decide whether the result appears common, unusual, or very unusual.

    1. A fair coin produces 7 heads in 10 flips.
    2. A fair coin produces 10 heads in 10 flips.
    3. A student randomly guesses on 20 true/false questions and gets 17 correct.

    1. The null hypothesis is that the coin is fair. The observed result is 7 heads in 10 flips. Simulate many sets of 10 fair flips and count how often 7 or more heads occur. This result is possible and fairly common, so it would usually be described as common or not especially surprising.

    2. The null hypothesis is that the coin is fair. The observed result is 10 heads in 10 flips. Simulate many sets of 10 fair flips and count how often all 10 are heads. This result is very unusual under the null hypothesis, although it is not impossible.

    3. The null hypothesis is that the student is guessing randomly, so each answer has a 50% chance of being correct. The observed result is 17 correct out of 20. Simulate many groups of 20 random true/false guesses and count how often 17 or more are correct. This result would generally be unusual or very unusual and may provide evidence that random guessing is not the whole explanation.

    Closure

    A researcher says, "This result is statistically significant, so chance could not have caused it." Do you agree? Explain.

    Statistical significance does not mean chance was impossible. It means the observed result would be unusual if chance alone, under the null hypothesis, were the explanation.

    One sentence to remember:

    "A statistically significant result is unusual under the null hypothesis, but unusual does not mean impossible or proven."

    Lesson 10

    Introducing P-Values

    Bellringer

    How unusual is unusual?

    A basketball player normally makes about 50% of their free throws. In a particular game, they make 9 out of 10.

    • If the player's true success rate is 50%, how surprising is 9 out of 10?
    • How could we use simulation to investigate?
    • Suppose we run 1,000 simulations and 52 produce 9 or more makes. What does 52 out of 1,000 tell us?
    • How could we turn "how surprising" into a number?
    • It is somewhat unusual because 9 makes is far above the expected 5 makes.
    • Simulate many sets of 10 free throws with a 50% success rate and compare the simulated results with 9 makes.
    • About 52 out of every 1,000 null-model simulations produced a result at least as extreme, so the estimated proportion is 0.052.
    • We can count the simulated results at least as extreme as the observed result and divide by the total number of simulations.

    Objectives

    • Explain what a p-valueThe proportion of results from the null model that are at least as extreme as the observed result. represents.
    • Calculate or estimate a p-value using simulation.
    • Interpret a p-value in context.
    • Explain how p-values provide evidence against a null hypothesis.
    • Explain why a small p-value does not prove that a claim is true or that one variable caused another.

    Explore: From Surprising to a Number

    Return to the Lesson 9 free-throw investigation. The observed result was 9 makes out of 10.

    Assume the null hypothesis: The player's true free-throw success rate is 50%.

    Suppose 1,000 simulated sets of 10 free throws are generated, and 52 simulations produce 9 or more makes.

    • What does each simulation represent?
    • Why are we interested in results of 9 or more rather than only exactly 9?
    • What proportion of simulations produced a result at least as extreme as the observed result?
    • What does that proportion tell us?
    • Would you consider 52 out of 1,000 to be a common or unusual result?
    • How could this proportion be used as evidence against the null hypothesis?

    p-value = the proportion of results from the null model that are at least as extreme as the observed result.

    For this example, the estimated p-value is 52 / 1000 = 0.052.

    The p-value measures how surprising the observed result is under the null hypothesis.

    • Each simulation represents one possible result if the player's true success rate were 50%.
    • Results of 9 or more are at least as extreme as 9, so counting them includes results even farther from what the null hypothesis predicts.
    • The proportion is 52 / 1000 = 0.052.
    • It estimates how often chance alone would produce a result at least this extreme under the null hypothesis.
    • It is an unusual result because only about 5.2% of the simulations were at least this extreme.
    • A small proportion provides evidence that the null hypothesis may not explain the observed result well, although it does not prove the null hypothesis false.

    Guiding Questions

    • What is the null hypothesis?
      The null hypothesis is the assumption about the ordinary or chance process being used for comparison.
    • What is the observed result?
      The observed result is the result actually seen in the data.
    • What does a p-value measure?
      A p-value is the proportion of null-model results at least as extreme as the observed result.
    • Why do we count results that are at least as extreme as the observed result?
      Counting all results at least as extreme measures how often chance produces a result this unusual or more unusual.
    • Why do we use a simulation to calculate a p-value?
      Simulation creates a null distribution showing what results chance alone can produce.
    • What does a small p-value suggest?
      A small p-value suggests the observed result is unusual under the null hypothesis and provides stronger evidence against it.
    • What does a large p-value suggest?
      A large p-value suggests the observed result is not especially unusual under the null hypothesis.
    • Does a small p-value prove the null hypothesis is false?
      No. It provides evidence against the null hypothesis but does not prove it false.
    • Does a p-value tell us the probability that the null hypothesis is true?
      No. A p-value is not the probability that the null hypothesis is true.
    • Why is p = 0.01 stronger evidence against the null hypothesis than p = 0.30?
      Results with p = 0.01 are less common under the null hypothesis than results with p = 0.30, so they provide stronger evidence against it.
    • What does statistical significance mean?
      Statistical significance means an observed result is sufficiently unusual under the null hypothesis to provide notable evidence against it.
    • Why is 0.05 commonly used?
      It is a commonly used guideline for judging evidence, not a universal law or proof threshold.
    • Does p = 0.06 prove that nothing is happening?
      No. It means the result did not meet that guideline; it does not prove that nothing is happening.
    • Does statistical significance prove causation?
      No. Statistical significance can show evidence of an association or difference, but it does not establish cause and effect.
    • Why should we interpret a p-value in the context of the original question?
      Context identifies the variables, comparison, null hypothesis, and practical meaning of the evidence.

    The Big Idea

    A p-value measures how unusual an observed result would be if the null hypothesis were true.

    We generate a null distributionThe distribution of results produced by randomization or simulation under the null hypothesis. through randomization or simulation, then determine the proportion of simulated results that are at least as extreme as the observed result.

    Small p-value → observed result is unusual under the null hypothesis → stronger evidence against the null hypothesis.

    Large p-value → observed result is not especially unusual under the null hypothesis → weaker evidence against the null hypothesis.

    A p-value is not the probability that the null hypothesis is true. A small p-value also does not prove causation.

    Closure

    A student says, "The p-value was 0.02, so there is only a 2% chance that the null hypothesis is true." Do you agree or disagree? Explain what the p-value actually means.

    One sentence to remember:

    "A p-value measures how surprising the data are under the null hypothesis, not how likely the null hypothesis is to be true."
