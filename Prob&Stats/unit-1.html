Lesson 1

What Is Statistical Thinking?

Bellringer

Do athletes get better grades?

  • Make an initial prediction. Do you think athletes get better grades than non-athletes?
  • What makes you think that?
  • What information would you want before making that conclusion?
  • Could two students have different answers even if they are both athletes?

Objectives

  • I can explain why variability matters when answering statistical questions.
  • I can identify individuals and variables in a data context.
  • I can distinguish between categorical and quantitative variables.
  • I can explain what makes a question a statistical question.

Explore

Do athletes get better grades?

We have been asked to investigate this question, but we have not been given any data yet. What data would we need to collect to answer this question?

Brainstorm what information would be useful before making a conclusion.


Examples include things like whether someone is an athlete, their grades, current classes, grade level, number of classes taken, or other student characteristics.

    Each row represents one individual student in the study.

  • What information is being recorded?

    We are recording information about each student's athlete status and GPA. Other information about the student could also be recorded.

  • What could we change about the information we collect?

    We could collect additional variables, such as grade level, number of classes, study time, hours of sleep, attendance, or other characteristics that might be related to grades.

  • Which pieces are categories?

    Athlete status is categorical because students can be placed into categories such as "Yes" or "No."

  • Which pieces are numerical measurements?

    GPA is a quantitative variable because it is recorded numerically and represents a measurement.

  • Is this enough information to answer the original question?

    It gives us a starting point, but we would probably want data from many students rather than just a few. We may also want additional variables that could help us understand differences in GPA.

  • What other variables might matter?

    Variables such as grade level, study time, sleep, attendance, number of classes, course difficulty, or other factors could potentially be related to GPA.

Guiding Questions

  • Why can't we simply look at one athlete and one non-athlete and decide whether athletes get better grades?

    Two students are not enough to represent all athletes and non-athletes. Individual students can be very different, so the result could simply be due to the particular students we happened to choose.

  • Why do we need many individuals?

    Looking at many individuals gives us more information about the variability in the population and makes our conclusions less dependent on a few unusual individuals.

  • Why is variability important?

    Students do not all have the same grades, study habits, schedules, or experiences. Because individuals vary, we cannot expect every athlete or every non-athlete to have the same GPA.

  • What information would be useful to collect about each student?

    We could collect athlete status, GPA, grade level, number of classes, study time, attendance, sleep, or other variables that might help explain differences in grades.

  • Which variables are categorical?

    Athlete status is categorical because students can be placed into groups such as "athlete" and "non-athlete." Other variables, such as grade level, could also be categorical depending on how they are recorded.

  • Which variables are quantitative?

    GPA is quantitative because it is represented by a numerical measurement. Other examples could include hours of sleep, number of classes, or study hours.

  • Why might two reasonable studies produce different results?

    Different studies might involve different students, different sample sizes, or different ways of collecting data. Because there is variability and randomness in data, two reasonable samples will not necessarily produce identical results.

  • Does finding a difference between two groups automatically mean that one thing caused the other?

    No. A difference between groups can be associated with other variables or differences between the groups. An observational study can show an association, but it does not automatically establish causation.

  • What makes "Do athletes get better grades?" a statistical question?

    The question requires collecting data from multiple students, and we expect the students' grades and other characteristics to vary. Because the answer depends on data and involves variability and uncertainty, it is a statistical question.

The Big Idea

Statistics answers questions under uncertainty.

Collecting Good Data

Bellringer

  • Suppose you wanted to determine which teacher students think is the best teacher in the school.
  • Who would you ask?
  • Would you ask every student in the school?
  • If you only asked 20 students, which 20 students would you choose?
  • Could two reasonable groups of 20 students give you different answers?

Objectives

  • I can explain why the way data are collected matters.
  • I can identify a population and a sample.
  • I can describe how a sample can be biased.
  • I can identify common sampling methods.
  • I can explain why random sampling is useful when collecting data.

Explore

Who is the best teacher in the school?

We want to determine which teacher students think is the best teacher in the school.

We don't have time to ask every student, so we decide to ask a sample of students.

Consider these possible ways to choose the students:

  • Method A: Ask the first 20 students who walk into school tomorrow morning.
  • Method B: Ask 20 students from the student council.
  • Method C: Ask 20 students who volunteer to complete a survey.
  • Method D: Randomly select 20 students from the entire school.

  • Which method would you trust the most?
  • Which method would you trust the least?
  • Could some students be more likely than others to be included in certain methods?
  • Could any method make one teacher look better or worse than another?
  • What would make a sample representative of the school?

Which method would you trust the most? Method D would generally be the most trustworthy because students are randomly selected from the entire school. This gives students throughout the school a chance to be included.

Which method would you trust the least? Method C could be especially problematic because students choose whether to participate. Students with particularly strong opinions about teachers may be more likely to volunteer.

Could some students be more likely than others to be included in certain methods? Yes. Method A favors students who arrive early, Method B only includes student council members, and Method C includes students who choose to volunteer. Different groups of students may therefore have different chances of being selected.

Could any method make one teacher look better or worse than another? Yes. If certain groups of students tend to prefer certain teachers, a sample that includes more of those students could make those teachers appear more or less popular than they would in the entire school.

What would make a sample representative of the school? A representative sample should reflect the population reasonably well in ways that matter to the question. Random sampling helps because it gives individuals throughout the population a chance to be selected rather than deliberately favoring a particular group.

Sampling Activity

Imagine your school had 1,000 students but you only had time to sample 50 of them. What are the pros and cons of selecting the following groups of 50?

  • 50 students chosen randomly from the entire school.
  • 50 students chosen from one grade level.
  • 50 students who volunteer.
  • 50 students chosen from the lunchroom.
  • 50 students chosen from the first students to arrive at school.

  • Who has a chance to be included?
  • Who might be left out?
  • What characteristics might be overrepresented?
  • What characteristics might be underrepresented?
  • Would you expect this sample to accurately represent the entire school?
  • How could the sample affect the conclusion?

Who has a chance to be included? In a random sample from the entire school, every student has a chance to be selected. In the other samples, the chance of selection depends on characteristics such as grade level, whether a student volunteers, where they eat lunch, or when they arrive at school.

Who might be left out? Students who do not belong to the selected grade, do not volunteer, eat somewhere other than the lunchroom, or arrive later could have little or no chance of being included in some samples.

What characteristics might be overrepresented? The sample may contain too many students with characteristics associated with the selection method. For example, a lunchroom sample might overrepresent students who regularly eat in that location.

What characteristics might be underrepresented? Students who do not fit the selection method may be underrepresented. For example, a sample of volunteers may underrepresent students who are less interested in completing surveys.

Would you expect this sample to accurately represent the entire school? A random sample from the entire school would generally be the most reasonable choice because every student has a chance to be selected. The non-random samples may systematically leave out certain groups.

How could the sample affect the conclusion? If the sample contains too many or too few students with certain characteristics, the results may differ from what we would find if we studied the entire school. This can lead to a biased conclusion.

Guiding Questions

  • What is the population in this investigation?

    The population is all 1,000 students in the school—the entire group we want to learn about.

  • What is the sample?

    The sample is the smaller group of students we actually select and survey, such as the 50 students chosen for the investigation.

  • Why might we use a sample instead of collecting data from everyone?

    Studying everyone may take too much time, money, or effort. A well-chosen sample can give us useful information about the larger population.

  • What does it mean for a sample to be representative?

    A representative sample reasonably reflects the population in characteristics that are important to the question. It should not systematically favor or leave out particular groups.

  • Why might a volunteer sample be biased?

    People who choose to volunteer may differ from people who do not volunteer. For example, students with especially strong opinions may be more likely to participate.

  • Why might asking only one grade level produce a misleading conclusion?

    Students in different grade levels may have different experiences, opinions, or characteristics. A sample from only one grade may therefore not represent the entire school.

  • What happens when certain individuals have little or no chance of being selected?

    Those individuals are underrepresented or completely left out of the sample. If the excluded individuals differ from those who are selected, the sample can be biased.

  • Why does randomly selecting individuals help us obtain a better sample?

    Random selection gives individuals a fair opportunity to be selected and reduces the chance that we intentionally or unintentionally favor a particular group.

  • Does a random sample guarantee that our sample will perfectly match the population?

    No. Random sampling reduces the risk of systematic bias, but random variation still exists. A random sample can differ from the population simply because of chance.

  • Could two random samples produce different results? Why?

    Yes. Random samples contain different individuals, so the results can vary from sample to sample. This is an example of variability.

  • If two samples produce different results, what does that tell us about variability?

    It shows that different samples can produce different results even when they are selected using the same method. Sampling variability is an important part of statistical reasoning.

  • Why is the way we collect data part of statistical thinking?

    The method we use to collect data affects which individuals are represented and therefore can affect our conclusions. Good statistical thinking considers how the data were obtained before using them to make conclusions.

The Big Idea

Good statistical conclusions depend on good data.

When we want to learn about a populationThe entire group of individuals we want to learn about., we often study a smaller sampleA smaller group of individuals selected from a population to collect data from.. The way we choose that sample matters because different sampling methods can produce different results. A random sampleA sample selected using a random process so that individuals have a fair chance of being selected. gives individuals a fair chance of being selected and helps reduce sampling biasA systematic problem in how a sample is selected that causes some individuals or groups to be overrepresented or underrepresented., but it does not eliminate variabilityThe fact that different individuals or samples can produce different results.. Different random samples can still produce different results.

Statistics is not just about analyzing data after we collect it. How we obtain the data can affect the conclusionsStatements or decisions we make based on evidence from data. we are able to make.

Explore

Is this coin fair?

Suppose someone claims that a coin is fair.

You flip the coin 10 times and get:

H H H H H H H T T H

You got 8 heads out of 10 flips.

  • Does this prove the coin is unfair?
  • If the coin really were fair, could you still get 8 heads?
  • What about 9 heads?
  • What about 10 heads?
  • How often would you expect to get 8 or more heads just by chance?

We need a way to investigate what could happen if the coin really is fair.

That is where simulation comes in.

Does this prove the coin is unfair? No. Getting 8 heads out of 10 flips could happen even with a fair coin. We need to know how often results like this happen just by chance before deciding that the coin is unusual.

If the coin really were fair, could you still get 8 heads? Yes. Randomness can produce groups of flips that are not exactly half heads and half tails.

What about 9 heads? Yes, 9 heads is possible with a fair coin, but it should happen less often than 8 heads.

What about 10 heads? Yes, 10 heads is also possible with a fair coin, but it should be even less common.

How often would you expect to get 8 or more heads just by chance? We could use a simulation of many groups of 10 fair coin flips and count how often 8 or more heads occur. This gives us an idea of how unusual 8 heads would be under the assumption that the coin is fair.

Did anyone get 10 heads? It is possible, but it is uncommon. Whether it appears in 100 simulations depends on random chance.

Does getting 8 heads now seem unusual? It is somewhat unusual because it occurs less often than results near 5 heads, but it is still possible under the assumption that the coin is fair.

How many simulations produced 9 or more heads? Usually very few. The exact number will vary because the simulations are random.

How does this compare with getting 8 or more heads? Getting 9 or more heads should be less common than getting 8 or more heads because there are fewer outcomes that qualify.

Which result would be more surprising? 9 or more heads is more surprising than 8 or more heads because it occurs less often in simulations of a fair coin.

What if we observed 10 heads out of 10? That would be very unusual under the assumption of a fair coin, although it would still be possible.

Does "possible" mean "not surprising"? No. Something can be possible but very unlikely. Simulation helps us distinguish between results that are common and results that are rare.

How often did random guessing produce 16 or more correct guesses? It should happen occasionally but not very often. The exact proportion will vary from simulation to simulation.

Is 16 correct surprising? It is somewhat surprising because it is much higher than the typical result for random guessing, but it is still possible by chance.

Would you be more convinced if the student got 18 correct? Yes. A score of 18 would be less common under random guessing, so it would provide stronger evidence that something unusual may be happening.

What if the student got 20 correct? Getting all 20 correct through random guessing would be extremely unusual. It would provide much stronger evidence against the idea that the student is simply guessing randomly.

What does our simulation tell us? It tells us how often results like the observed score can occur when the student is only guessing randomly. If the observed result is very rare in the simulation, we have evidence that random guessing may not adequately explain what happened.

Guiding Questions

  • Why did we begin by assuming that the coin was fair?

    We need a starting point for our simulation. Assuming the coin is fair lets us model what results could happen if nothing unusual is happening.

  • What does it mean to say that the coin is fair?

    A fair coin has an equal chance of landing heads or tails on each flip.

  • Why did we simulate many sets of 10 flips instead of flipping the coin only once?

    One set of 10 flips gives us only one result. Many simulations show us the range of results that could happen just by chance and help us see which results are common or unusual.

  • What does each simulation represent?

    Each simulation represents one possible set of results that could occur if the coin is fair or if the student is simply guessing randomly.

  • Why can a fair coin still produce 8 heads out of 10 flips?

    Randomness does not guarantee exactly half heads and half tails in every small group of flips. An outcome such as 8 heads can happen by chance.

  • Why isn't every possible result equally likely?

    Some numbers of heads can occur in more combinations of individual flips than others. For example, there are many different sequences that produce 5 heads, while only one sequence produces 10 heads.

  • What makes a result unusual or surprising?

    A result is unusual when it occurs relatively rarely under the model or assumption we are investigating.

  • Why is 10 heads out of 10 more surprising than 6 heads out of 10?

    A fair coin produces results near 5 heads more often than extreme results such as 10 heads. Therefore, 10 heads is much less common.

  • What does the simulation tell us about what can happen just by chance?

    It shows us the range of results that can occur when nothing unusual is happening and helps us determine which results are common and which are rare.

  • Why do we need many simulations?

    Many simulations give us a better picture of the distribution of possible results. A small number of simulations could make a rare event appear more or less common just because of random variation.

  • What is the purpose of starting with the assumption that "nothing unusual is happening"?

    It gives us a model for what we would expect if there were no real effect or unusual process. We can then compare the observed result with what the model produces.

  • How is this similar to the random sampling we studied in Lesson 2?

    In both situations, randomness creates variability. Different random samples can produce different results, just as different random simulations can produce different results.

  • Why doesn't an unusual result automatically prove that something unusual is happening?

    Even unlikely events can happen by chance. An unusual result provides evidence against our assumption, but it does not prove that the assumption is false.

  • If a result is very unlikely under our assumption, what might that tell us?

    It may suggest that the assumption does not adequately explain what happened and that something other than ordinary chance may be involved.

The Big Idea

Chance modelsModels that describe what results might occur because of random chance. help determine what is surprising.

Randomness creates variabilityThe fact that different individuals, samples, or random outcomes can produce different results., even when nothing unusual is happening. We can use randomizationUsing a random process to represent or produce chance outcomes. and simulationA model that uses a random process to imitate a real situation. to model what results might occur just by chance. To do this, we begin with a null ideaThe starting assumption that there is no real effect, difference, or unusual process.: assume that there is no real effect, difference, or unusual process happening.

We then compare an observed resultThe actual result that we obtained from the situation being studied. to the results produced by our simulation. If the observed result is common in the simulation, it is not particularly surprising. If the observed result is very unusual, we have evidenceInformation from data or simulations that supports or challenges an idea. that the original assumption may not explain what happened.

A simulation does not prove that something is true or false. Instead, it helps us answer an important statistical question:

"Could we reasonably get a result like this just from chance?"

Would You Be Surprised?

For each situation, decide whether the result would be not surprising, somewhat surprising, or very surprising if nothing unusual were happening.

  1. A fair coin is flipped 10 times and lands on heads 6 times.
  2. A fair coin is flipped 10 times and lands on heads 10 times.
  3. A six-sided die is rolled 20 times and lands on 6 exactly 3 times.
  4. A six-sided die is rolled 20 times and lands on 6 exactly 15 times.
  5. A student guesses randomly on 20 true/false questions and gets 11 correct.
  6. A student guesses randomly on 20 true/false questions and gets 19 correct.

Choose one of the situations. State what you would assume if nothing unusual were happening, describe how you could simulate that situation, and explain what result would make you suspicious that something unusual was happening.

1. Fair coin, 6 heads out of 10: Not surprising. Results close to 5 heads are common when flipping a fair coin.

2. Fair coin, 10 heads out of 10: Very surprising. It is possible with a fair coin, but it is extremely uncommon.

3. Six-sided die, 3 sixes in 20 rolls: Not surprising. Getting a few sixes in 20 rolls is a reasonable result when the die is fair.

4. Six-sided die, 15 sixes in 20 rolls: Very surprising. A fair die would rarely produce that many sixes.

5. Random guessing, 11 correct out of 20: Not surprising. Random guessing should produce results around half correct, and 11 is very close to that.

6. Random guessing, 19 correct out of 20: Very surprising. Getting 19 correct by random guessing would be extremely rare.

How often do you get 0 or 10 heads? Both are possible but very rare. In 1,000 simulations, you may see one or both, but it is also possible not to see either.

How often do you get 8 or more heads? It should occur occasionally but much less often than results near 5.

Does the computer simulation look similar to what we saw in Lesson 3? Yes. Both should show that results near 5 heads are common and extreme results are uncommon.

Why is a computer useful for this investigation? A computer can perform thousands of simulations quickly and organize results into a graph.

What would be difficult about performing 1,000 simulations by hand? It would take a very long time and create more opportunities for recording or calculation errors.

Investigation 3: Change the Simulation

Now change the simulation. Instead of 10 flips, simulate 50 flips. Compare the results to the simulation with 10 flips.

  • What changed?
  • Is there still variability?
  • Does the proportion of heads tend to stay near 50%?
  • Are extreme results more or less common?
  • Why?

What changed? With 50 flips, the total number of heads varies over a larger numerical range, but the proportion tends to stay near 50%.

Is there still variability? Yes. Randomness still causes different simulations to produce different results.

Does the proportion of heads tend to stay near 50%? Yes. Individual simulations can differ.

Are extreme results more or less common? Extreme proportions become less common as the number of flips increases.

Why? With more flips, random variation has less effect on the overall proportion, so results become more stable.

Guiding Questions

  • Why might a graph be more useful than a list of numbers?

    A graph organizes the data visually so that patterns, clusters, gaps, variability, and unusual observations can be easier to see.

  • What information can a graph help us see quickly?

    A graph can show the center, spread, shape, clusters, gaps, and possible unusual observations.

  • Why is it useful to have technology calculate numerical summaries for us?

    Technology can calculate summaries quickly and accurately, especially when there are many observations. This allows us to spend more time interpreting what the numbers mean.

  • Does using technology mean we no longer need to understand the statistics? Why or why not?

    No. Technology can perform calculations, but we still need to decide what to calculate, whether the method is appropriate, and what the results mean.

  • Why is it important to choose an appropriate graph?

    Different graphs emphasize different features of data. A graph should match the type of variable and make important characteristics easier to see.

  • What makes one graph more useful than another for a particular question?

    The best graph clearly displays the features relevant to the statistical question and the type of data being analyzed.

  • Why is technology especially useful when we have a large data set?

    Technology can organize, summarize, graph, and analyze thousands or millions of observations much faster than we could by hand.

  • Why is technology useful for simulation?

    Computers can repeat random processes thousands of times very quickly, allowing us to study variability and likelihood.

  • What would happen if we tried to run 10,000 simulations by hand?

    It would take a very long time and be impractical. Technology allows us to perform the repetitions quickly and consistently.

  • Does a computer simulation remove randomness? Why or why not?

    No. The simulation still uses randomness. The computer simply repeats and organizes random processes more efficiently.

  • What does each simulated result represent?

    Each simulated result represents one possible outcome that could occur under the assumptions of the simulation.

  • Why might two graphs of the same data look different?

    Different graph types emphasize different features. The same data might look different in a dotplot, histogram, or boxplot.

  • Can technology tell us what conclusion to make? Why or why not?

    No. Technology provides calculations, graphs, and evidence, but statistical reasoning is needed to decide what the evidence means.

  • What is the difference between using technology to calculate something and using technology to investigate something?

    Calculating means asking technology to produce a numerical answer. Investigating means using technology to explore data, look for patterns, compare possibilities, and use evidence to answer a statistical question.

  • How does technology allow us to ask statistical questions that would be difficult to answer by hand?

    Technology lets us work with large data sets, perform complicated calculations, create visualizations, and run many simulations quickly.

The Big Idea

Statistics today is computationalInvolving the use of computers or technology to perform calculations, organize information, or analyze data..

Modern statisticians use technology to organize, visualize, analyze, and simulate data. Computers can perform calculations and simulations much faster than people can, allowing us to focus more on reasoningUsing evidence and logical thinking to decide what the data suggest. and interpretationExplaining what statistical results mean in the context of the original question..

Technology does not replace statistical thinking. We still need to decide what question we are asking, what data we need, what analysisExamining data using graphs, numerical summaries, or other methods to find patterns and understand variability. is appropriate, and what the results mean.

A statistical investigationA process of using data to answer a statistical question. often follows this process:

Question → DataInformation collected about individuals or observations. → Graph → Analysis → Conclusion

Technology helps us move through this process efficiently so that we can spend more time thinking about the evidenceInformation from data that supports or challenges a conclusion..
