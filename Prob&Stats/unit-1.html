Lesson 1

What Is Statistical Thinking?

Bellringer

Do athletes get better grades?

  • Make an initial prediction. Do you think athletes get better grades than non-athletes?
  • What makes you think that?
  • What information would you want before making that conclusion?
  • Could two students have different answers even if they are both athletes?

Objectives

  • I can explain why variability matters when answering statistical questions.
  • I can identify individuals and variables in a data context.
  • I can distinguish between categorical and quantitative variables.
  • I can explain what makes a question a statistical question.

Explore

Do athletes get better grades?

We have been asked to investigate this question, but we have not been given any data yet. What data would we need to collect to answer this question?

Brainstorm what information would be useful before making a conclusion.


Examples include things like whether someone is an athlete, their grades, current classes, grade level, number of classes taken, or other student characteristics.

Technology / Simulation / Data Investigation

Data Planning Activity: Consider the following sample data table.

Student Athlete? GPA
Student A Yes 3.7
Student B No 3.1
Student C Yes 2.9
Student D No 3.8
  • What does each row represent?

    Each row represents one individual student in the study.

  • What information is being recorded?

    We are recording information about each student's athlete status and GPA. Other information about the student could also be recorded.

  • What could we change about the information we collect?

    We could collect additional variables, such as grade level, number of classes, study time, hours of sleep, attendance, or other characteristics that might be related to grades.

  • Which pieces are categories?

    Athlete status is categorical because students can be placed into categories such as "Yes" or "No."

  • Which pieces are numerical measurements?

    GPA is a quantitative variable because it is recorded numerically and represents a measurement.

  • Is this enough information to answer the original question?

    It gives us a starting point, but we would probably want data from many students rather than just a few. We may also want additional variables that could help us understand differences in GPA.

  • What other variables might matter?

    Variables such as grade level, study time, sleep, attendance, number of classes, course difficulty, or other factors could potentially be related to GPA.

Guiding Questions

  • Why can't we simply look at one athlete and one non-athlete and decide whether athletes get better grades?

    Two students are not enough to represent all athletes and non-athletes. Individual students can be very different, so the result could simply be due to the particular students we happened to choose.

  • Why do we need many individuals?

    Looking at many individuals gives us more information about the variability in the population and makes our conclusions less dependent on a few unusual individuals.

  • Why is variability important?

    Students do not all have the same grades, study habits, schedules, or experiences. Because individuals vary, we cannot expect every athlete or every non-athlete to have the same GPA.

  • What information would be useful to collect about each student?

    We could collect athlete status, GPA, grade level, number of classes, study time, attendance, sleep, or other variables that might help explain differences in grades.

  • Which variables are categorical?

    Athlete status is categorical because students can be placed into groups such as "athlete" and "non-athlete." Other variables, such as grade level, could also be categorical depending on how they are recorded.

  • Which variables are quantitative?

    GPA is quantitative because it is represented by a numerical measurement. Other examples could include hours of sleep, number of classes, or study hours.

  • Why might two reasonable studies produce different results?

    Different studies might involve different students, different sample sizes, or different ways of collecting data. Because there is variability and randomness in data, two reasonable samples will not necessarily produce identical results.

  • Does finding a difference between two groups automatically mean that one thing caused the other?

    No. A difference between groups can be associated with other variables or differences between the groups. An observational study can show an association, but it does not automatically establish causation.

  • What makes "Do athletes get better grades?" a statistical question?

    The question requires collecting data from multiple students, and we expect the students' grades and other characteristics to vary. Because the answer depends on data and involves variability and uncertainty, it is a statistical question.

The Big Idea

Statistics answers questions under uncertainty.

Statistical thinkingUsing data and evidence to answer questions when there is variability and uncertainty. involves asking questions that anticipate variabilityThe fact that individuals or observations are not all the same., collecting or obtaining appropriate dataInformation collected about individuals or observations., understanding individualsThe people, objects, or other cases being studied. and variablesCharacteristics or measurements recorded about individuals that can have different values., looking for patternsFeatures or trends in data that help us understand what is happening. while recognizing that data vary, using evidenceInformation from data that supports or challenges a conclusion. to make reasonable conclusionsStatements or decisions we make based on evidence from data., and recognizing that conclusions are uncertainNot known with complete confidence because variability or randomness can affect the results. rather than absolute. Statistics is not simply a collection of formulas.

Apply

Generate your own statistical question related to something you are interested in, such as sleep, sports, video games, music, social media, school, weather, or food.

For your question, identify:

  1. The individuals you would study.
  2. At least two variables you would collect.
  3. Which variables are categorical.
  4. Which variables are quantitative.
  5. What variability you expect to see.

Closure

After today's investigation, what would you need to know before you would trust an answer to the question, "Do athletes get better grades?"

In one or two sentences, explain what you think statistical thinking means.
